# Critical Speed / Critical Power

## Summary

Critical Speed (CS) is the highest running speed that can be sustained in a metabolic
**steady state** — the boundary between the *heavy* and *severe* intensity domains,
below which VO2, lactate and pH can stabilise and above which they drift inexorably to
exhaustion. CS (its cycling analogue is Critical Power, CP) is paired with a finite
work-capacity term, **D′** (distance runnable above CS; W′ in cycling), and together
they define a hyperbolic speed–duration curve that predicts how long any supra-CS pace
can be held. The single most important coaching takeaway: **CS is a hard physiological
ceiling on sustainable pace — durations above it are metered out of a fixed tank (D′)
that empties, so any pace above CS has a finite, predictable time-to-exhaustion**, while
pace below CS is sustainable for far longer and largely limited by fuel and durability,
not by this mechanism.

## What it is

For any individual, the relationship between exercise intensity and the time it can be
held is, in the severe domain, a near-perfect **hyperbola**. Fit a hyperbola to several
maximal efforts and two parameters fall out:

- **Critical Speed (CS)** — the asymptote (in m/s, or km/h, or as a pace in sec/km). It
  is the highest intensity at which physiological systems reach a steady state. In
  cycling the equivalent asymptote is **Critical Power (CP)**, in watts.
- **D′** (read "D-prime", in metres) — the *curvature constant*: a fixed finite distance
  that can be covered **above** CS before exhaustion, regardless of how far above. In
  cycling this is **W′** ("W-prime", a quantity of work in joules/kilojoules). It behaves
  like a battery: spent fast at high speeds, slowly just above CS, and (partly)
  recharged when you drop back below CS.

CS sits between the first lactate/ventilatory threshold (LT1/VT1, the easy–moderate
boundary) and VO2max. It is functionally the runner's "threshold" in the modern,
physiologically-defined sense — the heavy/severe boundary, very close to the maximal
lactate steady state (MLSS) and the second lactate turnpoint (LT2). For most runners CS
corresponds to a pace they could race for roughly **30–60 minutes** all-out — broadly
the 10 km to half-marathon range for trained runners.

**Typical values** (field-test reviews; population matters a great deal):

- Recreational runners: **~3.4–4.2 m/s** (≈ 4:00–4:54 / km).
- Trained / sub-elite distance runners: **~4.3–4.8 m/s** (≈ 3:28–3:53 / km).
- Elite marathoners: **~4.9–5.0 m/s** (≈ 3:20–3:24 / km); elite track runners higher.
- Female distance runners (Pettitt 3MT cohort): **~4.46 ± 0.41 m/s**.
- D′ in runners typically **~150–300 m**; W′ in cyclists typically **~15–30 kJ**.

[Lipková 2025; Pettitt 2012; Galbraith 2014]

## Physiology / mechanism

**Why a hyperbola, and why a hard boundary.** Below CS, the rate of oxidative ATP supply
can fully meet demand: VO2 reaches a steady state, blood lactate stabilises (production
≈ clearance), and there is no obligatory progressive loss of homeostasis — exercise can
continue for a long time. **At and above CS this steady state becomes impossible.** VO2
no longer plateaus but rises with time (the "VO2 slow component") until it reaches
VO2max; intramuscular phosphocreatine falls, inorganic phosphate and H⁺ accumulate, and
fatigue-related metabolites build progressively. CS is therefore the *highest metabolic
rate that is fully sustainable by oxidative metabolism* [Jones 2010].

**D′ / W′ as the finite reserve.** The work done above CS is supported by a limited store
— originally conceived as "anaerobic work capacity" (substrate-level phosphorylation,
PCr breakdown, glycolysis to lactate) but better understood today as the integral of the
homeostatic disturbance you can tolerate: the amount of supra-CS work achievable before
the metabolic milieu (Pi, H⁺, depleted PCr, attainment of VO2max) forces task failure.
When D′ is fully expended, exhaustion occurs — at any speed above CS, exhaustion
coincides with the *same* end-point metabolic state and with the attainment of VO2max
[Jones 2010; Jones & Vanhatalo 2017].

This gives the model real predictive teeth: time-to-exhaustion above CS is simply how
long it takes to drain D′ at the rate the chosen overspeed demands. Two runners with the
same CS but different D′ will have very different 800 m–3 km performances; the one with
the larger D′ can hold a higher fraction above CS for longer.

**Reconstitution.** Below CS, D′/W′ is partially restored over time (the basis of
interval and intermittent-exercise pacing models), though reconstitution is slower and
less complete than depletion and is itself an area of active modelling [Jones &
Vanhatalo 2017].

## The evidence

- **[Established]** The speed/power–duration relationship in the severe domain is
  hyperbolic, described by two parameters (CS/CP asymptote and D′/W′ curvature
  constant), and this form is highly conserved across species, exercise modes and muscle
  groups. The governing equation is `t = D′ / (v − CS)` [Jones 2010; Jones & Vanhatalo
  2017].

- **[Established]** Above CS/CP, no metabolic steady state exists: VO2 drifts to VO2max,
  PCr falls and metabolites accumulate progressively, and exhaustion at *every* supra-CS
  intensity occurs at a common end-point state and at VO2max. Below CS these variables
  stabilise [Jones 2010].

- **[Established]** CS/CP demarcates the **heavy/severe intensity-domain boundary** and
  lies very close to MLSS and LT2 — i.e. it is the physiologically correct "threshold"
  for prescribing the top of sustainable work [Jones 2010; Micheli 2025].

- **[Probable]** CP is *systematically a little higher* than MLSS rather than identical.
  Meta-analysis (10 studies, 111 participants, predominantly cycling): CP exceeded
  MLSS power by a mean **12.4 W** (95% CI 4.7–20.2 W), but with **wide limits of
  agreement (−19 to +44 W)**; no
  difference in VO2, HR or blood lactate at the two intensities. The gap is **larger in
  fitter and younger** athletes and is sensitive to test methodology, so CP and MLSS are
  **not freely interchangeable at the individual level** [Micheli 2025].

- **[Established]** The **3-minute all-out test (3MT)** estimates CS/CP from the mean
  speed/power of the final 30 s, and D′/W′ from the work done above that end value. In
  cycling, end-test power correlates with CP at **r = 0.99** and work-above-end with W′ at
  **r = 0.84** [Vanhatalo 2007].

- **[Probable]** The **running 3MT** gives valid CS estimates: in female runners
  CS = 4.46 ± 0.41 m/s did not differ from treadmill threshold speed (4.55 ± 0.24 m/s),
  and predicted 1600 m and 5000 m performance (ICC 0.74 and 0.87). It is appropriate for
  events lasting ≈ **2.5–18 min**; it under-predicts very short events (e.g. 800 m)
  [Pettitt 2012].

- **[Probable]** **Multi-distance / multi-duration field tests** (2–3 maximal time
  trials) reliably estimate CS. A single-visit field test (3 runs of 3600, 2400, 1200 m)
  matched multi-day treadmill CS, with a typical error of **0.14 m/s for CS** and **88 m
  for D′** — note D′ is far noisier than CS [Galbraith 2014; Lipková 2025].

- **[Emerging]** The **anaerobic term (D′/W′) is the weak link**: the 3MT under-estimates
  D′ by ~16% and, in tethered/non-motorised treadmill running, work-above-end did **not**
  correlate well with conventionally-modelled W′ (r ≈ 0.37–0.52). Treat CS as robust and
  D′ as an approximate, noisy parameter [Gama 2018; Lipková 2025].

- **[Contested]** Whether the hyperbolic CP model is the *best* description of endurance,
  or whether a **power-law** model fits the broader intensity–duration range better, is
  genuinely debated; critiques also question the physiological "infinite sustainability"
  framing of CP. The two-parameter hyperbola remains the dominant, practically useful
  model within the severe domain, but it is not the final word [Dotan 2022; Black 2022].

## How we compute it

`@daud/core` ownership: **not yet computed** (proposed `criticalSpeed` / `dPrime`).

**Core hyperbolic model** (severe domain). For a set of maximal efforts where effort *i*
covers distance `dᵢ` in time `tᵢ`:

- Speed–time form: `v = CS + D′ / t`
- **Linear distance–time form** (preferred for fitting): `d = CS · t + D′`
  → regress distance on time; **slope = CS**, **intercept = D′**. Most robust.
- Tolerable duration above CS at pace `v > CS`: `t = D′ / (v − CS)`.

**Estimation methods, in rough order of practicality:**

1. **Multi-distance time trials (recommended for runners).** 2–3 maximal efforts, each
   **3–12 min** long (e.g. ~1200 m, ~2400 m, ~3600 m), on the same surface, with full
   recovery (30–60 min) between, ideally longest-to-shortest. Fit the linear `d = CS·t + D′`.
   Three trials beat two for precision [Galbraith 2014; Lipková 2025].
2. **3-minute all-out test (3MT).** One maximal effort at constant, high resistance/
   effort; **CS = mean speed of final 30 s**, **D′ = distance covered above CS**. Time-
   efficient but demands true all-out pacing from the gun and yields a noisier D′
   [Pettitt 2012; Vanhatalo 2007].
3. **From race results / habitual hard efforts.** CS can be back-fitted from two or more
   recent maximal races of differing duration in the 3–~30 min band — convenient and
   "free", but only as good as the efforts were truly maximal and comparable in
   conditions [Lipková 2025].

**Error & ground truth.** CS is the reliable parameter (field typical error ≈ 0.14 m/s,
CV ~3%); **D′ is the noisy one** (typical error ~88 m; CV ~5% and method-dependent up to
~16% bias). Estimates drift with trial duration: using only short trials inflates CS.
None of these are "lab ground truth" — MLSS/LT2 from a graded lactate test is the nearest
reference, and even that differs from CP by a variable margin [Micheli 2025].

## How the coach uses it

CS is the anchor of the runner's **pace zones** and the key to honest pacing advice.

**Zone anchoring (all stages).**

- **Below CS (heavy/moderate):** sustainable work. Tempo / threshold running sits at or
  just below CS; easy/long runs sit well below (near LT1). These are "sustainable
  forever-ish" relative to the CS mechanism — limited by fuel, heat and durability, not
  by D′.
- **At CS:** the redline of steady-state running — roughly a 30–60 min race effort. Use
  as the ceiling for "comfortably hard" continuous work.
- **Above CS (severe):** every pace has a finite time-to-exhaustion `t = D′/(v − CS)`.
  This is interval territory. Reviews report aerobic-stimulus intervals are commonly
  prescribed at **~110–130% of CS** [Lipková 2025].

**Pacing & race prediction.** For a target race, check the required average pace against
CS. If it is above CS, the race must fit within the D′ budget — useful for flagging
"you've gone out too hard": if a runner banks supra-CS pace early, D′ depletes and a
positive split / blow-up is predictable. Trained marathoners pace **below** CS (~85–95%
of CS); the shorter the race, the higher the sustainable fraction of/above CS.

**By stage.**

- **Stage 1 (beginner):** Do **not** field-test CS with all-out trials early. Estimate CS
  conservatively from easy-run data or a recent hard effort and use it only to keep easy
  running *easy* (well below CS). Avoid 3MT/time-trial protocols until the runner is
  conditioned. Confidence in any beginner CS estimate is low.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** Introduce a proper field test (multi-distance TTs preferred;
  3MT as a time-efficient alternative). Use CS to set tempo and to design supra-CS
  intervals at 110–130% CS. Re-test every ~6–12 weeks; CS should rise with training.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** Use CS + D′ together for race-pace selection, pacing strategy and
  interval prescription; model session "cost" as D′ spent and recovered. Track CS as the
  primary fitness trend and D′ as a (noisier) measure of speed reserve.

**Cross-checks.** Treat CS as an *estimate to be individualised from the runner's own
data*, not gospel. Reconcile it with HR at threshold, RPE, and the lactate-threshold
doc. In heat, on hills, on soft ground or at altitude, the *sustainable pace* drops below
flat-road CS even though the underlying CS hasn't changed — weight effort/RPE and HR,
not just the pace number. If race performances consistently beat or miss CS-based
predictions, re-fit CS from those races.

## Honesty & uncertainty

- **CS ≠ MLSS exactly.** CP/CS tends to slightly *over*-estimate the true maximal
  steady-state intensity, by a small and individually variable margin (mean ~12 W in
  cycling, wide LoA, bigger in fitter/younger athletes). Prescribing long continuous
  efforts at exactly CS may push some runners just into the unsustainable domain
  [Micheli 2025].
- **D′/W′ is genuinely noisy and model-dependent.** It varies with the test, the chosen
  trial durations, and the math (linear vs hyperbolic fit). The 3MT under-estimates it,
  and in some running modes it doesn't agree with classic models at all. Use D′
  qualitatively (big vs small speed reserve), not as a precise joule/metre count
  [Gama 2018; Lipková 2025].
- **Test quality dominates.** CS estimates are only as good as the efforts: sub-maximal
  trials, poor pacing in a 3MT, mismatched surfaces/wind/temperature, or trials outside
  the 3–12 min sweet spot all bias the result. Short trials inflate CS [Lipková 2025].
- **Day-to-day and within-person variation.** Fatigue, glycogen status, heat, sleep and
  motivation move test outputs. A single test is a snapshot; trend across several.
- **Population gaps.** Much of the foundational CP work is in cycling and in male,
  trained cohorts; female- and beginner-specific data are thinner, and CS norms vary
  widely by training status [Lipková 2025].
- **Model is debated at the edges.** The hyperbolic two-parameter model is excellent
  *within* the severe domain over ~2–~30 min but is challenged by power-law alternatives
  and critiqued for its "infinitely sustainable below CP" idealisation; real sustainable
  duration below CS is still finite (fuel, heat, durability) [Dotan 2022; Black 2022].
- **What's still unknown:** the true physiological basis and reconstitution kinetics of
  D′/W′, how CS shifts acutely with fatigue/durability late in long races, and the best
  single field protocol across abilities.

## Safety bounds

- **No supra-CS continuous prescription for beginners.** Stage 1 runners should not be
  sent on all-out CS field tests or sustained severe-domain efforts; estimate CS
  conservatively from easy data. (Mirrored as a stage guardrail in `@daud/core`.)
- **Treat CS as a ceiling, not a target, for long sustained efforts.** Because CS can sit
  slightly above MLSS, prescribe long tempo/threshold work *at or just below* estimated
  CS, never above, unless deliberately doing intervals.
- **Cap interval intensity by D′, not bravado.** Supra-CS interval volume must respect the
  finite D′ budget and adequate recovery; do not stack severe-domain work without
  recovery that allows D′ reconstitution.
- Otherwise no hard physiological danger limits specific to this metric beyond general
  high-intensity-exercise and overtraining safeguards.

## Bottom line

- **Act on confidently (conclusive):**
  - The speed/power–duration relationship in the severe domain is **hyperbolic**, defined
    by two parameters — the **CS/CP asymptote** and the **D′/W′ curvature constant** — and
    this form is robust and conserved across modes and species [Jones 2010; Jones &
    Vanhatalo 2017].
  - **CS is a genuine physiological boundary** (heavy/severe): below it VO2, lactate and
    pH can stabilise; at/above it they drift to exhaustion, which coincides with VO2max
    and a common end-point metabolic state [Jones 2010].
  - Time-to-exhaustion above CS follows **`t = D′/(v − CS)`** — supra-CS pace is a finite,
    predictable withdrawal from a fixed tank, so over-fast starts and unrealistic goal
    paces are detectable in advance.
  - **CS is the robust, reliable parameter** to anchor pace zones; field typical error
    ≈ 0.14 m/s (CV ~3%) [Galbraith 2014; Lipková 2025].
  - Estimating CS from **maximal-effort tests** is sound in principle, and the **cycling
    3MT is well-validated** (end-test power ≈ CP, r ≈ 0.99) [Vanhatalo 2007]. The
    **running-specific protocols** (running 3MT, single-visit field TTs) are usable but
    only **Probable, not conclusive** — see "Hold loosely" [Pettitt 2012; Galbraith 2014].

- **Hold loosely (unsettled):**
  - **CS/CP is *not* identical to MLSS** — it sits slightly above (mean ~12 W in cycling,
    wide limits of agreement, larger gap in fitter/younger athletes), so the two are not
    interchangeable at the individual level [Micheli 2025; Nixon 2021].
  - **D′/W′ is noisy and model-dependent**: the 3MT under-estimates it, some running modes
    disagree with classic models entirely, and it should be used qualitatively (large vs
    small reserve), not as a precise joule/metre count [Gama 2018; Lipková 2025].
  - **Whether the hyperbola is the best model is debated** at the edges — power-law
    alternatives fit the broader duration range better, and the "infinitely sustainable
    below CP" idealisation is critiqued; the hyperbola holds *within* ~2–30 min severe-
    domain efforts only [Dotan 2022; Black 2022].
  - **Running-specific test validity is Probable, not established**: the running 3MT rests
    largely on one small cohort (n = 14 female runners), and single-visit field TTs on
    limited validation; use them, but re-fit CS from the runner's own races [Pettitt 2012;
    Galbraith 2014; Lipková 2025].
  - **Female-, beginner- and running-specific norms are thinner** than the male/cycling
    foundation; treat published CS/D′ values as priors to individualise from the runner's
    own data [Lipková 2025].

## Coach Directives

- **D1:** Anchor pace zones to estimated CS — easy/long well below CS, tempo at/just
  below CS, intervals above CS. — confidence: Established.
- **D2:** Model time-to-exhaustion for any pace above CS as `t = D′ / (v − CS)`; use it to
  flag unsustainable goal paces and over-fast starts. — confidence: Established.
- **D3:** Treat pace **below CS as sustainable** (limited by fuel/heat/durability) and pace
  **above CS as a finite withdrawal from D′**. — confidence: Established.
- **D4:** Prefer **2–3 maximal time trials of 3–12 min each** (e.g. 1200/2400/3600 m, full
  recovery, longest-first) to estimate CS; fit linear `d = CS·t + D′`. (The linear
  work–time model is canonical, but single-visit field-test validity rests on limited
  cohorts.) — confidence: Probable.
- **D5:** Accept the **3-min all-out test** as a time-efficient alternative: CS = mean
  speed of final 30 s, D′ = distance above CS; valid for events ~2.5–18 min. — confidence:
  Probable.
- **D6:** Trust **CS as robust; treat D′ as noisy** and use it only qualitatively (large vs
  small speed reserve). — confidence: Probable.
- **D7:** Because CS can slightly exceed MLSS, prescribe sustained continuous efforts
  **at or just below** CS, never above. — confidence: Probable.
- **D8:** Prescribe aerobic-power intervals at **~110–130% of CS**, capped by available D′
  and recovery. — confidence: Probable.
- **D9:** Re-estimate CS every **~6–12 weeks** and after notable fitness change; track CS
  as the primary fitness trend and re-fit from race results when predictions miss. —
  confidence: Probable.
- **D10:** Discount the flat-road CS pace in heat, on hills/soft ground, or at altitude —
  weight RPE and HR for the sustainable pace there. — confidence: Established.
- **D11 (safety):** Do **not** prescribe all-out CS field tests or sustained severe-domain
  efforts to Stage 1 beginners; estimate CS conservatively from easy-run data. —
  confidence: Established (safety guardrail).
- **D12:** Communicate CS/D′ as **individual estimates to refine from the runner's own
  data**, never as fixed truth; hedge language to the evidence grade. — confidence:
  Established.
