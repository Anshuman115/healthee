# Running Economy

## Summary
Running economy (RE) is the energy demand of running at a constant submaximal
speed — how much oxygen (or energy) it costs a runner to hold a given pace. It is
one of the three pillars of endurance performance alongside VO₂max and the
lactate/critical-speed threshold, and crucially it is the one that best separates
runners who are otherwise alike: **among athletes with similar VO₂max, running
economy can vary by ~30% and explains a large share of who actually races faster**
(Established) [Barnes & Kilding 2015a; Conley & Krahenbuhl 1980]. The single most
important coaching takeaway: a "smaller engine" (VO₂max) runner can beat a bigger
engine by being more economical, so the coach should value the interventions that
improve economy — consistent aerobic volume over months, heavy/explosive strength
work, and advanced footwear — and never judge fitness on VO₂max alone. RE is also
trainable across an entire career, unlike VO₂max, which plateaus early.

## What it is
Running economy is defined as **the steady-state oxygen uptake (VO₂) required to
run at a given submaximal velocity** [Barnes & Kilding 2015a]. A more economical
runner consumes less oxygen — and therefore less metabolic energy — to hold the
same pace, leaving more aerobic headroom and pushing back the point at which they
must recruit costly anaerobic metabolism.

It is expressed three main ways:

- **O₂ per unit time** — mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ at a fixed reference speed (16 km·h⁻¹ /
  3:45 per km is the most common comparison velocity).
- **O₂ per unit distance** — mL·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹ (the oxygen "cost of transport"), which
  is more honest across speeds because it normalises for how far you travelled.
- **Energy per unit distance** — kcal·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹, derived from VO₂ *and* the
  respiratory exchange ratio (RER). This is the most physiologically correct
  expression, because the energy yield per litre of O₂ depends on fuel mix
  (carbohydrate vs fat); two runners with identical VO₂ but different RER do not
  have identical *energy* cost [Fletcher et al. 2009].

Representative norms at the common 16 km·h⁻¹ reference speed (from Barnes &
Kilding's pooled literature; lower = more economical). Cross-study comparison is
genuinely difficult, so read the *spread and direction*, not the exact figure
[Barnes & Kilding 2015a]:

| Population | Approx. RE (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| Recreational | ~48–55 (often tested at lower speeds, 12–14 km·h⁻¹) |
| Moderately trained | ~50–55 |
| Highly trained | ~50–51 (highly trained males average ≈50.6 at 16 km·h⁻¹) |
| Elite | ~45–48 (elite males average ≈47.9 at 16 km·h⁻¹; the most economical sub-45) |

## Physiology / mechanism
RE is the net output of the whole movement-and-metabolism chain, which is why
Barnes & Kilding call it "a complex, multifactorial concept." Four interacting
systems determine it [Barnes & Kilding 2015a; Saunders et al. 2004]:

1. **Metabolic / muscular efficiency** — how efficiently the muscle turns chemical
   energy into mechanical work (ATP per O₂, mitochondrial coupling, fibre-type
   distribution, substrate use). Training may raise mitochondrial efficiency
   (higher ATP/O₂, less uncoupled respiration).
2. **Cardiorespiratory efficiency** — the O₂ cost of breathing and circulating.
   The work of ventilation alone accounts for a meaningful slice of submaximal
   VO₂; improved ventilatory efficiency is one route by which RE improves with
   training.
3. **Biomechanical efficiency** — converting muscular power into forward motion
   with minimal waste: anthropometrics (a smaller Achilles-tendon moment arm
   stores more elastic energy and correlates strongly with better RE; slim,
   distally-light lower legs cost less to swing), stride parameters (runners
   self-optimise stride length near their metabolic minimum), vertical
   oscillation, and ground-contact mechanics.
4. **Neuromuscular efficiency** — refined motor programming, optimal leg/vertical
   stiffness, and an effective stretch-shortening cycle. Trained runners show less
   wasteful muscle co-activation than novices.

**Elastic energy is central.** During each stance, tendons and the arch act as
springs that store and return mechanical energy: Barnes & Kilding estimate the
Achilles tendon recycles ~35% and the foot arch ~17% of the kinetic + potential
energy per step, and that without this elastic recoil the oxygen cost of running
would be on the order of ~30–40% higher [Barnes & Kilding 2015a]. Anything that
improves spring-like recoil (tendon stiffness, neuromuscular stiffness regulation,
or a midsole that stores and returns energy) lowers the metabolic bill. This is the
mechanistic through-line linking strength training, plyometrics, and carbon-plate
footwear: all three act, at least partly, on the storage-and-return spring system.

## The evidence

- **[Established]** RE discriminates performance among similar runners, often more
  than VO₂max. *Seminal primary study* [Conley & Krahenbuhl 1980]: 12 highly
  trained males of comparable ability (mean VO₂max 71.7 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, all top-19
  in a 10 km race, mean 32.1 min). With VO₂max effectively held constant, steady-
  state VO₂ (running economy) at 268 m·min⁻¹ correlated **r = 0.82 with 10 km time
  — ~67% of the performance variance** in this homogeneous group. This is the
  foundational demonstration that within a pack of similar engines, the more
  economical runner wins. Replicated since in trained cohorts [Saunders et al.
  2004].
- **[Established]** RE varies widely between runners of equal aerobic capacity.
  *Systematic review of the literature* [Barnes & Kilding 2015a]: **running economy
  can differ by as much as ~30% among trained runners with similar VO₂max** — a gap
  easily large enough to flip finishing order.
- **[Established]** The "performance VO₂" framework: endurance performance is set by
  VO₂max (engine size), the sustainable fraction of it (threshold/critical speed),
  *and* running economy (speed per unit O₂) — not by any one alone [Joyner & Coyle
  2008]. Economy multiplies the engine size you already have.
- **[Established]** Heavy/explosive strength training improves RE in trained
  runners. *Meta-analysis of controlled trials* [Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2016]:
  an overall **large, beneficial effect** on RE in highly trained middle- and
  long-distance runners; the effective recipe was low-to-high-load resistance plus
  plyometrics, 2–3×/week for 8–12 weeks. A larger, more recent *meta-analysis (652
  runners, GRADE moderate)* refines it by method [Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024]:
  **high-load training** SMD −0.27 (p = 0.039; small, larger at higher speeds and
  higher VO₂max); **combined methods** SMD −0.43 (moderate); **plyometrics**
  non-significant overall but beneficial at speeds ≤12 km·h⁻¹; **submaximal-load and
  isometric** training not significant. So the *direction* is settled (strength
  helps), the *magnitude* is small-to-moderate and method-dependent.
- **[Established]** Advanced footwear technology ("super shoes": compliant
  high-resilience foam + embedded carbon plate) improves RE. *Seminal crossover*
  [Hoogkamer et al. 2018]: prototype Nike Vaporfly reduced the energetic cost of
  level running by **~4% on average** vs established racing shoes — the origin of
  the "4%" name. *Meta-analysis of crossover trials* [Kobayashi et al. 2025]:
  pooled RE improvement **−5.34 mL·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹ (≈ −2.9%)**. The mean effect is real
  and consistent in *direction*; see Honesty for the large individual spread.
- **[Probable]** RE is trainable across a whole career, unlike VO₂max which
  plateaus early. *Consistent observational and training-study evidence* [Saunders
  et al. 2004; Barnes & Kilding 2015b]: training history and accumulated volume are
  among the strongest correlates of good RE, and economy continues to improve with
  years of running even after VO₂max stops rising. Meaningful improvements accrue
  over **months to years**, not weeks — slow but durable.
- **[Probable]** Optimal biomechanics are individual, not universal. *Review of
  kinematic studies* [Barnes & Kilding 2015a; Moore 2016]: correlations between
  individual joint angles/velocities and RE are mostly trivial-to-moderate, and "an
  economical change in one athlete may be uneconomical in another." There is no
  single textbook form most economical for everyone — though some modifiable factors
  (reducing overstride/excess vertical oscillation, allowing self-selected stride
  length) trend favourable.
- **[Emerging]** Altitude/hypoxic training may improve RE via skeletal-muscle
  metabolic adaptation. *Small training-camp studies, mixed and confounded*: some
  show RE gains after live-high blocks, but samples are small, controls weak, and
  the mechanism (mitochondrial efficiency) is inferred rather than proven. Promising
  but not settled.
- **[Contested]** Which specific gait cue to coach for economy. Because the
  individual-optimum finding above is robust, blanket form prescriptions (e.g.
  forefoot striking for everyone, a fixed cadence) are *not* supported as economy
  improvers; the evidence conflicts and effects are runner-specific. See the cadence
  and running-form-metrics docs.

## How we compute it
**Lab ground truth.** RE is measured by indirect calorimetry: steady-state
submaximal VO₂ (and ideally RER for energy cost) collected over several minutes at
one or more fixed sub-threshold speeds on a treadmill or track. Best practice is to
report energy cost (kcal·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹) using both VO₂ and RER, and to measure below
the lactate threshold so metabolism is genuinely aerobic and steady [Fletcher et
al. 2009].

**Field proxies (estimates, not measurements).** Daud has no gas analyser, so true
RE is **not yet computed** in `@daud/core`. Usable proxies from wearable data:

- **Pace-at-a-fixed-HR / HR-at-a-fixed-pace** trend — if a runner holds a faster
  easy pace at the same heart rate over weeks (with stable conditions), that is
  consistent with improving economy *and/or* improving fitness; the two are
  confounded and cannot be cleanly separated without lab gas exchange.
- **Vendor "running power" and efficiency metrics** (e.g. power-to-pace, watch
  "running economy" scores) are modelled estimates with no oxygen measurement; treat
  as noisy trends only.

Flag clearly: any field "economy" signal blends true metabolic economy with
fitness, fatigue, heat, terrain, and GPS error. It is a trend, never a lab value.

## How the coach uses it
**General stance:** treat RE as a *long-horizon, trainable* lever and a corrective
to VO₂max worship. When a runner's VO₂max is flat but their race times or
pace-at-HR keep improving, that is economy (and threshold) doing its job — frame it
as progress, not stagnation.

- **Stage 1 (beginner):** Do not measure or chase RE directly. The dominant economy
  driver here is simply **accumulated easy aerobic volume and consistency** — the
  neuromuscular and biomechanical refinements that come free with months of running.
  Coach consistency; expect economy (and pace-at-HR) to improve quietly.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** Introduce **strength training** as a deliberate economy
  intervention — heavy resistance and/or plyometrics 2–3×/week, 8–12+ weeks, kept
  separate enough from key runs to manage fatigue [Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2016;
  Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024]. Expect small-to-moderate, slow-building gains. Continue
  to read improving pace-at-HR as economy progress.
- **Stage 3 (racing/trained):** Economy is now the main lever, because VO₂max is
  largely capped. Prioritise (a) sustained strength/plyometric work, (b) **advanced
  footwear for races and key sessions** (≈2–4% energy saving on average), and (c)
  protecting the high-volume aerobic base that underpins economy. Frame race goals
  around predicted race time, not VO₂max.

**Cross-check rules:**
- Never declare a runner "not improving" off a flat VO₂max alone — check pace-at-HR,
  threshold pace, and durability, which carry economy gains.
- When comparing two runners or two paces, normalise economy talk to a fixed speed
  and remember heat, hills, and fuelling all shift submaximal O₂/HR.
- Strength work is an *economy and durability* tool, not a substitute for running
  volume; prescribe it within the load/recovery guardrails (it adds fatigue).

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **We cannot measure true RE from a watch.** Field "economy" is inseparably
  confounded with fitness, fatigue, hydration, heat, terrain, shoe, and GPS pace
  error. A nicer pace-at-HR is *consistent with* better economy but does not prove
  it. Daud must phrase economy claims as trends, not measurements.
- **Cross-study norms are soft.** Protocol, gas system, averaging window, and test
  speed all move RE numbers, so the published norm tables carry real noise; use
  relative change within one runner/protocol, not absolute cross-runner cutoffs
  [Barnes & Kilding 2015a].
- **Optimal form is individual.** The data do not support a universal "economical
  technique." Joint-kinematics–RE correlations are mostly weak, and a change that
  helps one runner can hurt another [Barnes & Kilding 2015a; Moore 2016]. Be wary of
  confident form prescriptions.
- **Super-shoe response varies a lot between people.** The *mean* is ~2–4% better,
  but individual responses in the literature range from large benefit to small
  *impairment* (reports spanning roughly +10% to −13% RE change across studies and
  individuals). There are responders and non-responders at every level, and the
  plate's effect cannot be cleanly separated from the foam/geometry [Hoogkamer et
  al. 2018; Kobayashi et al. 2025]. Recommend a try-before-racing test rather than
  promising every runner 4%.
- **Strength-training magnitude is modest and method-dependent.** Direction is
  established; the effect is small-to-moderate (SMD roughly −0.3 to −0.4) and only
  clearly significant for high-load and combined methods — isometric/submaximal
  loading showed no RE benefit [Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024]. Don't oversell it.
- **Altitude and economy is unsettled** (small, confounded studies), and the energy
  vs O₂ distinction means RER/fuel changes can make VO₂-only economy look better or
  worse without a true efficiency change [Fletcher et al. 2009].
- **Mechanisms remain partly "poorly understood"** — even Joyner & Coyle flag the
  determinants of economy as incompletely mapped, despite economy's clear
  performance importance [Joyner & Coyle 2008].

## Safety bounds
None intrinsic to RE as a metric. The indirect caveat: the main RE interventions
carry load. **Heavy strength training and plyometrics add neuromuscular fatigue and
injury exposure** and must be progressed within the engine's existing load/recovery
guardrails (ACWR ceiling, readiness/HRV flags, progression limits) — introduce
gradually, separate from hard runs, and defer when readiness is red. Advanced
footwear changes lower-limb loading patterns; rotate it in gradually rather than
debuting it on a long race day. A desire to "improve economy" must never justify
exceeding load or progression limits.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Among runners with similar VO₂max, the more economical runner tends to race
  faster; economy often discriminates performance better than VO₂max [Conley &
  Krahenbuhl 1980; Barnes & Kilding 2015a]. Value and develop it.
- RE varies ~30% between similar-VO₂max runners and is a genuine, independent
  performance determinant [Barnes & Kilding 2015a; Joyner & Coyle 2008].
- Heavy/explosive strength training improves RE in trained runners (high-load and
  combined methods; small-to-moderate effect) [Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2016;
  Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024].
- Advanced (carbon-plate) footwear improves RE by ~2–4% on average [Hoogkamer et al.
  2018; Kobayashi et al. 2025].
- RE improves with accumulated aerobic training over months-to-years, and keeps
  improving after VO₂max plateaus — *graded Probable (consistent observational and
  training-study evidence, not RCT-conclusive); treat the direction as dependable,
  the "after-plateau" specifics as likely-but-unproven* [Saunders et al. 2004;
  Barnes & Kilding 2015b].

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- The exact magnitude any individual gets from super shoes (responders vs
  non-responders; some get little or worse) — test, don't promise.
- Which gait/form cue improves a given runner's economy — optimal mechanics are
  individual; avoid universal form prescriptions.
- Altitude/hypoxia as an economy intervention (small, confounded evidence).
- Any field "economy" reading as a true efficiency measure rather than a
  fitness-confounded trend.

## Coach Directives
- **D1:** Treat running economy as a core performance determinant on par with VO₂max
  and threshold; among similar runners, prefer the more economical one and develop
  economy deliberately. — confidence: Established
- **D2:** Never judge a runner's progress or potential on VO₂max alone; if VO₂max is
  flat while pace-at-HR, threshold pace, or durability improve, credit economy and
  report progress. — confidence: Established
- **D3:** Prescribe heavy-resistance and/or plyometric strength training (≈2–3×/week,
  ≥8–12 weeks) as an economy-and-durability intervention for developing and trained
  runners — within load/recovery guardrails. — confidence: Established
- **D4:** Expect strength-training economy gains to be small-to-moderate and
  method-dependent (favour high-load and combined methods; do not rely on isometric
  or light submaximal loading for RE). — confidence: Probable
- **D5:** Recommend advanced (carbon-plate) footwear for races and key sessions for
  an expected ~2–4% energy saving — but advise testing it in training first, since
  individual response varies widely. — confidence: Established (mean) / Emerging
  (individual)
- **D6:** Frame economy improvement as a months-to-years process driven chiefly by
  consistent aerobic volume; in beginners, build economy via consistency, not via
  measurement or form drills. — confidence: Probable
- **D7:** Do not prescribe a universal "economical" running form (fixed cadence,
  forced forefoot strike, etc.); optimal mechanics are individual — adjust only clear
  inefficiencies and watch the runner's own response. — confidence: Contested
- **D8:** Treat any wearable/field "economy" signal (pace-at-HR, running power) as a
  fitness-confounded trend, never a measured efficiency value; control for heat,
  terrain, and fatigue before interpreting. — confidence: Established
- **D9:** Introduce strength, plyometrics, and new footwear gradually and within
  load/readiness guardrails; defer added load when readiness is poor. — confidence:
  Established (safety mirror)

## Healthee implementation & honesty policy
- **Not currently computed.** Healthee derives no running-economy field. True RE
  requires **steady-state submaximal O₂ measurement** (a metabolic cart), which no
  wearable — including the Helio strap — provides; Healthee has no O₂ signal.
  (This note has no `daud_metrics` provenance to drop.) It is **reference science +
  a weak future-metric candidate** (`applies_to_metrics: []`).
- **Future-metric candidate (proxy only, not true RE).** From existing GPS pace +
  interpolated strap HR (`derive/gps.py`) Healthee could compute an
  **efficiency-factor-style proxy** (speed per heartbeat on a standardised easy
  effort) and track its *trend*. That is **not** running economy — it is an HR-cost
  surrogate confounded by everything HR is — and must never be labelled as measured
  RE. Note that the submaximal-VO₂max estimator (`derive/vo2max_submax.py`) already
  models VO₂ from speed+grade, so a related O₂-cost proxy is conceptually reachable
  but still an estimate, not lab RE.
- **Population: runners.** RE is a running-specific efficiency construct.
- **Honesty rules (carry into any future UI + the coach today):**
  - **RE is lab-grade; wearable proxies are not interchangeable with measured O₂
    cost** — never present a speed-per-heartbeat number as "your running economy".
  - RE is **individual and trainable over a whole career**; among runners of
    similar VO₂max it helps explain who races faster — frame it as a long-horizon
    trend, not a day-to-day readout.
