# Cadence (Step Rate)

## Summary
Cadence is how many steps you take per minute (spm). The popular "everyone should
run at 180 spm" rule is a **myth** — it traces to a single elite-race observation,
not a prescription, and healthy optimal cadence is highly individual (it rises with
speed and falls with height/leg length). What *is* well-supported: nudging an
individual runner **+5–10% above their own habitual cadence** shortens the stride,
reduces overstriding and braking, and lowers per-step joint loads — most reliably at
the knee/patellofemoral joint (peak PFJ force ≈ −14% at +10%, [Lenhart 2014]). The
coaching move is a *relative* nudge from the runner's own baseline, never a forced
march to an absolute number.

## What it is
**Cadence (step rate)** = steps per minute, counting both feet. **Stride rate** is
sometimes quoted as full gait cycles (one per leg), so 180 spm = 90 strides/min —
watch for this factor-of-two ambiguity in older literature, which often counts a
single leg or arm-swing.

Cadence and **step length** are the two multiplicative components of speed:

> speed = cadence × step length

At any given pace you can run with higher cadence + shorter steps, or lower cadence
+ longer steps. Cadence is not fixed — it climbs naturally as you speed up.

Typical ranges (both feet, easy-to-moderate running):
- **Recreational runners:** ~150–185 spm; many habitually sit ~160–170.
- **Trained/sub-elite:** ~170–185 at moderate pace.
- **Elite at race pace:** often ~180–200+, but with a genuinely wide spread.

Even among elite runners the range is large. In 20 elite ultramarathoners over a
100-km road race, mean step frequency was **182 spm but ranged 155.4–203.1 spm**,
and within-race changes were driven almost entirely by *speed*, not by ability
[Burns 2019]. Equally fast, equally elite athletes self-select very different
cadences.

## Physiology / mechanism
Why a *relative* cadence increase changes loading:

1. **Shorter step → foot lands closer under the body.** Higher cadence at constant
   speed shortens step length, which reduces **overstriding** — landing with the
   foot well ahead of the centre of mass (COM) on a more extended knee.

2. **Less braking.** A foot planted ahead of the COM produces a horizontal
   ground-reaction force pointed backward (a braking impulse) that the body must
   then re-accelerate against every step. Pulling the foot-strike back under the
   COM reduces **braking impulse** and horizontal "stop-go" cost [Heiderscheit 2011].

3. **Less vertical bounce.** Shorter steps lower **vertical COM excursion**, so
   there is less downward momentum to arrest at landing [Heiderscheit 2011].

4. **Lower per-step joint work, especially at the knee.** With a less-extended knee
   at contact, peak knee flexion and the quadriceps demand that drives
   patellofemoral-joint (PFJ) compression both drop. Peak stance-phase knee flexion
   is the single largest predictor of PFJ load (R² ≈ 0.68) and explains most of the
   PFJ-force reduction seen when cadence rises [Lenhart 2014].

5. **The trade-off — more steps.** Higher cadence means *more* loading cycles per
   kilometre. The net injury benefit depends on whether the drop in **per-step
   strain magnitude** outweighs the rise in **cycle count**. Modelling of tibial
   stress fracture suggests strain magnitude dominates, so shorter strides still net
   out protective for bone — but the margin is modest [Edwards 2009].

There is also a **metabolic optimum**: plotting energy cost (or heart rate) against
cadence at a fixed speed gives a shallow U [van Oeveren 2017]. A runner's
*self-selected* cadence sits **near** the bottom of that U but, for many
(especially inexperienced) runners, *slightly below* it — i.e. a touch lower than
the energetically optimal cadence [van Oeveren 2017; de Ruiter 2020]. This is why a
*small* increase can help some runners while a *large* forced change away from
preferred tends to *raise* energy cost.

## The evidence

- **[Myth]** "Everyone should run at 180 spm." The number comes from coach/
  physiologist Jack Daniels counting stride rates at the **1984 Los Angeles
  Olympics**, where all but one of ~46 distance runners he observed were at ≥180 spm
  [Daniels, *Running Formula*]. This was an **observation of elite athletes racing
  at elite speeds**, never a controlled finding and never a target for recreational
  runners at training pace. A statistical floor for one population became a universal
  prescription — that leap is unsupported. There is no peer-reviewed study showing
  180 spm is optimal for, or should be imposed on, general runners.

- **[Established]** Optimal/habitual cadence is **individual and rises with speed.**
  Self-selected cadence increases with running speed and decreases with leg length /
  height; for many recreational runners the metabolically optimal cadence at easy
  pace is **below 180 spm** [van Oeveren 2017; Burns 2019]. Self-selected cadence
  sits near — usually *slightly below* — each runner's own metabolic optimum: in 12
  runners assessed outdoors, freely chosen step frequency was a few spm under the HR-
  derived energetic optimum, and inexperienced runners had the most to gain from a
  small increase [van Oeveren 2017; de Ruiter 2020, n=12]. The corollary holds in both
  directions: there is *no* single population-optimal cadence, and the right target is
  defined relative to the individual's own speed-specific habitual value.

- **[Probable]** A **+5–10% increase from a runner's own baseline** reduces
  overstriding-related mechanics: shorter step length, less braking impulse, lower
  vertical COM excursion, reduced peak knee flexion and peak hip adduction, and less
  knee/hip energy absorption (knee negative work ≈ −20% at +5%, ≈ −34% at +10%), with
  **minimal change in metabolic cost for increases ≤10%** [Heiderscheit 2011, n=45
  recreational runners, treadmill, ±5/±10%]. The 2022 meta-analysis (37 studies)
  confirms: strong evidence for reduced peak knee flexion (SMD 0.66), moderate
  evidence for reduced step length (0.93), hip adduction (0.40), knee extensor moment
  (0.50) and foot-strike angle (0.62) [Anderson 2022].

- **[Probable]** Increasing cadence **lowers patellofemoral-joint load.** At +10%
  step rate, peak PFJ force fell **14%**, PFJ loading rate **11%**, and per-stance
  impulse **20%**; cumulative PFJ impulse per *unit distance* still fell ~5.5%
  despite the extra steps [Lenhart 2014, n=30, musculoskeletal modelling]. Peak
  stance-phase knee flexion was the dominant predictor and drove the effect.

- **[Probable]** Shortening step length lowers patellofemoral-joint **stress**
  (force per unit cartilage area). Modelling a **16% step-length reduction** cut
  **peak PFJ stress ~22% per step** and **PFJ stress per unit distance ~7.5% per
  mile** in female runners with and without PFP [Willson 2014, n=10 PFP + 13
  controls]. Note a +10% cadence corresponds to only a ~9% shorter step, so a
  realistic +10% cadence move yields a *smaller* per-distance benefit than these
  16%-reduction headline figures. This per-step *stress* figure — not peak *force* —
  is the likely true source of the widely-quoted "≈26% PFJ reduction"; the most
  defensible single number for the **force** at +10% cadence remains **−14%** [Lenhart
  2014]. We cite the verified figures and avoid the inflated "26%/14% tibial" headline
  (see Honesty).

- **[Emerging]** Step-rate retraining may help **patellofemoral pain.** In runners
  with PFP, a +10% step-rate intervention reduced pain (≈6/10 → <1/10) and improved
  function at 4 weeks and 3 months, with reduced contralateral pelvic drop and hip
  adduction [Bramah 2019, n=12 single-arm feasibility study — low-level evidence but
  consistent with mechanism]. The 2022 meta-analysis likewise found only *limited*
  evidence of pain/function gains in PFP [Anderson 2022]. Graded **Emerging** (not
  Probable): a single uncontrolled n=12 study plus "limited" meta-analytic evidence
  cannot support a confident grade.

- **[Emerging]** **Low** cadence is associated with **bone-stress-injury (BSI)
  risk.** In a prospective cohort of 54 collegiate cross-country runners, each
  **+1 spm was associated with ~5% lower BSI risk** (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.91–0.98);
  step rate was the strongest predictor after sex and injury history [Kliethermes
  2021]. This is observational (association, not proven causation), but biologically
  coherent.

- **[Contested]** Whether higher cadence reliably reduces **tibial impact shock /
  loading** is *not* settled. A meta-analysis of gait interventions found increasing
  cadence had **no statistically significant effect on tibial load** (SMD −0.25, 95%
  CI −0.88 to 0.37 — a small, non-significant trend) [Keast 2022], and the broader
  step-rate meta-analysis found **no significant pooled effect on vertical loading
  rate or peak tibial acceleration** [Anderson 2022]. Modelling of *stride-length*
  reduction does predict a 3–6% drop in tibial stress-fracture probability for a 10%
  shorter stride [Edwards 2009]. So: bone benefit is plausible and epidemiologically
  supported, but the acute "impact shock" reduction is inconsistent and overstated in
  popular sources. **Treat "cadence cuts tibial shock ~14%" as not robustly
  established** — the well-replicated, large effect is at the knee/PFJ, not the
  tibia.

- **[Probable]** **Direct injury-prevention evidence is thin.** No high-quality RCT
  yet shows that raising cadence prevents injury in healthy runners; the strongest
  meta-analysis concluded there is *insufficient evidence* to determine cadence's
  effect on injury [Anderson 2022]. The case for cadence is built on mechanism +
  PFP rehab + BSI association, not on prevention trials.

- **[Probable]** Cadence changes ≤10% do **not** meaningfully harm running economy;
  larger forced changes (toward an arbitrary 180) move runners *off* their metabolic
  optimum and raise energy cost and perceived effort/awkwardness [van Oeveren 2017;
  Anderson 2022].

## How we compute it
Cadence = steps per minute, from the watch/phone accelerometer or footpod
(both feet). For analysis Daud uses two derived quantities:

- **Cadence vs. speed:** because cadence rises with pace, it is only interpretable
  *relative to the same runner at the same pace*. Never compare a runner's easy-run
  cadence to someone else's race cadence.
- **Cadence fade (within-run split):** `@daud/core` owns `CadenceSplit`
  (`firstHalfSpm`, `secondHalfSpm`) and the **`cadence-fade`** flag in `flags.ts`,
  which fires when cadence drops more than **5%** from the first to second half of a
  run (`CADENCE_FADE_FRACTION = 0.05`) — a fatigue/form signal, not an absolute
  target.

There is **no absolute optimal-cadence formula** in core, by design — the
individualised target is "the runner's own habitual cadence, optionally nudged
+5–10%," refined from their data, not a fixed constant.

## How the coach uses it
Decision logic, by **stage**:

- **All stages — never prescribe 180 (or any absolute number) as a goal.** Cadence
  guidance is always *relative to the runner's own baseline at a comparable pace*.

- **Stage 1 (beginner):** Do not touch cadence first. Consistency, easy effort and
  aerobic base matter far more than form tinkering. Only mention cadence if there is
  a clear problem (see below). If cited at all, frame it as "your own number," not a
  target.

- **Stage 2 (developing):** If the runner has **overstriding signs or a relevant
  injury** — patellofemoral pain, recurrent shin/tibial issues, audible heavy heel
  landing, very low cadence for their pace — suggest a **+5–10% nudge from their own
  habitual cadence**, cued with a metronome or playlist BPM, introduced gradually
  over weeks on easy runs. Expect benefit primarily at the knee/PFJ.

- **Stage 3 (racing):** Cadence naturally rises at race pace; do not impose a number.
  Use the **`cadence-fade`** flag — a >5% drop in the back half flags fatigue or
  pacing that ran long/hard, useful for pacing feedback, *not* a form fault to
  "fix" mid-race.

- **Cross-checks:** Cadence is meaningless without pace context. A "low" cadence at
  easy pace can be perfectly fine. Weight cadence alongside step length, injury
  history and economy — and never trade a runner's comfortable economy for an
  arbitrary target.

Confidence calibration: speak **plainly** that 180-for-everyone is a myth and that
optimal cadence is individual (Established). Speak with **light hedging** about the
benefits of a +5–10% self-relative nudge for the knee/PFJ and for at-risk runners
(Probable). **Flag the uncertainty** on tibial shock and on injury *prevention* in
healthy runners (Contested / insufficient evidence).

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **The "180" origin is an anecdote, not data.** It was elite athletes, at race
  pace, counted by eye at one event. It should never anchor a recommendation.
- **The headline numbers people quote are partly mismatched to the science.** The
  robust, replicated effect of +10% cadence is **−14% peak PFJ force** [Lenhart
  2014]; the often-repeated "−26% PFJ / −14% tibial shock (Heiderscheit)" figures are
  not what that primary study shows. The "≈26%" almost certainly comes from
  patellofemoral *stress* (force per area) under shorter steps — Willson 2014 reported
  ~22% per step / ~7.5% per mile for a 16% shorter step, a *different* quantity from
  peak force (and a larger step change than a +10% cadence move) — and the
  "−14% tibial" figure is frequently mis-pinned to Heiderscheit, which actually
  reported knee *energy absorption* −20%/−34% at +5/+10% and did not establish a 14%
  tibial-shock cut. PFJ *stance impulse* fell ~20% and per-distance impulse only ~5.5%;
  **tibial shock/loading effects were non-significant in meta-analysis** [Keast 2022;
  Anderson 2022]. We report the verified numbers and flag the rest.
- **Cadence trades strain for cycles.** More steps = more loading cycles. The net
  injury effect is favourable only if per-step strain falls enough — modelled as a
  small (3–6%) bone benefit [Edwards 2009], not a large one.
- **Individual variation is huge.** Equally elite runners span ~155–203 spm [Burns
  2019]; optimal cadence depends on speed, leg length, mass distribution, strength
  and gait. There is no population number that is "correct."
- **Self-selected ≈ metabolically near-optimal — but not exactly.** Forcing a runner
  far from their preferred cadence usually *raises* energy cost and feels awkward
  [van Oeveren 2017]. The nuance: self-selected cadence often sits a few spm *below*
  the energetic optimum, so a *small* increase can be neutral-to-helpful for some
  (especially novices) [van Oeveren 2017; de Ruiter 2020] — but big overhauls can
  trade one problem (overstriding) for another (calf/Achilles overload from forefoot
  loading, or wasted economy).
- **Day-to-day and within-run noise.** Cadence drifts with fatigue, terrain, grade,
  shoes, music and GPS/accelerometer error. A single-run cadence is a weak signal;
  trends and within-run fades are more informative than absolute readings.
- **Causation is unproven for prevention.** The BSI and PFP findings are association
  / rehab evidence; no strong RCT shows raising cadence *prevents* injury in healthy
  runners [Anderson 2022]. We should not promise injury prevention.
- **Measurement caveat:** stride vs. step (×2) confusion and single-leg counts make
  cross-study and cross-device comparison error-prone.

## Safety bounds
- **Hard rule: never prescribe an absolute cadence target (e.g. 180 spm).** Cadence
  directives are always relative to the runner's own baseline.
- **Cap any cued cadence change at +5–10% of the runner's habitual cadence**, phased
  in gradually. Larger acute jumps raise metabolic cost, feel unnatural and can shift
  load to the calf/Achilles or forefoot.
- For a runner **currently injured**, cadence retraining is an adjunct, not a
  substitute for appropriate load management and clinical care — defer to the
  injury/load-recovery docs and to a clinician for diagnosed injuries.

## Bottom line

- **Act on confidently (conclusive):**
  - **"180 spm for everyone" is a myth.** It traces to Jack Daniels eyeballing stride
    rates of elite distance runners *racing* at the 1984 LA Olympics — an observation
    of one fast population, never a controlled finding or a prescription for
    recreational runners at training pace [Daniels]. Correct it plainly [Myth].
  - **Optimal cadence is individual and rises with speed** (and falls with height/leg
    length). Equally elite runners span ~155–203 spm; within-runner change is driven by
    *speed*, not ability [Burns 2019]. There is no universal correct number
    [Established].
  - **Express cadence only relative to the runner's own habitual value at a comparable
    pace.** Cross-runner or cross-pace comparisons are meaningless [Established].
  - A **+5–10% nudge from the runner's own baseline** shortens stride, pulls the
    foot-plant back under the centre of mass, and reduces braking impulse, vertical
    bounce and per-step knee/patellofemoral load — most robustly at the knee
    (energy absorption −20%/−34% at +5/+10% [Heiderscheit 2011]; peak PFJ force −14% at
    +10% [Lenhart 2014]) — with little metabolic penalty for increases ≤10% [Probable].
  - **Cap any cued change at +5–10% and phase it in gradually.** Larger acute jumps
    raise energy cost, feel unnatural, and can shift load to the calf/Achilles
    [Heiderscheit 2011; Anderson 2022] — safety bound [Established as a bound].

- **Hold loosely (unsettled):**
  - Whether higher cadence reduces **tibial impact shock / loading** — meta-analyses
    find **no significant pooled effect** on tibial load or vertical loading rate
    [Keast 2022; Anderson 2022]. The popular "−14% tibial shock / −26% PFJ" headline
    overstates and mis-attributes the science [Contested].
  - Whether raising cadence **prevents injury in healthy runners** — no high-quality
    RCT; the case rests on mechanism + PFP rehab + a BSI *association* (each +1 spm ≈
    5% lower BSI risk, RR 0.95, observational [Kliethermes 2021]), not prevention
    trials [Probable evidence base is thin → treat as unproven].
  - The **size of the bone benefit** — modelling gives only a ~3–6% drop in tibial
    stress-fracture probability for a 10% shorter stride, partly offset by more loading
    cycles [Edwards 2009; Emerging].
  - **Patellofemoral-pain relief** from step-rate retraining — promising but supported
    mainly by small/low-level studies and *limited* meta-analytic evidence [Bramah
    2019; Anderson 2022; Emerging].

## Coach Directives
- D1: Never recommend 180 spm (or any fixed absolute cadence) as a target for any
  runner; correct the "180 myth" gently when it comes up. — confidence: Myth-corrected / Established
- D2: Express cadence only relative to the runner's own baseline at a comparable
  pace; never compare cadence across runners or across paces. — confidence: Established
- D3: When suggesting a cadence change, prescribe **+5–10% above the runner's own
  habitual cadence**, never more, phased in gradually on easy runs. — confidence: Probable (safety bound)
- D4: Prioritise a cadence nudge for runners with **patellofemoral pain,
  overstriding signs, or unusually low cadence for their pace**; expect benefit
  mainly at the knee/PFJ (≈ −14% peak PFJ force at +10%). — confidence: Probable
- D5: In Stage 1 beginners, do not introduce cadence work unless there is a clear
  overstriding/injury problem; base, consistency and easy effort come first. — confidence: Probable (practitioner consensus)
- D6: Use the `cadence-fade` flag (>5% first-half→second-half drop) as a fatigue/
  pacing signal, not as a form fault or an absolute-target violation. — confidence: Probable
- D7: Do **not** claim higher cadence reduces tibial/impact shock or prevents
  injury in healthy runners; the evidence there is non-significant/insufficient.
  Frame bone benefit (BSI association, modelled stress-fracture risk) as
  plausible-but-modest. — confidence: Contested / honest-uncertainty
- D8: Do not force a runner far from their self-selected cadence — it tends to raise
  energy cost and perceived effort; preserve economy unless overstriding/injury
  justifies a small nudge. — confidence: Probable
