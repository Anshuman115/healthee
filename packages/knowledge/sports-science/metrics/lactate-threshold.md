# Lactate Threshold (LT1, LT2, LTHR)

## Summary

Lactate threshold is the exercise intensity above which blood lactate begins to
accumulate faster than the body can clear it. It comes in two physiologically
distinct flavours — **LT1** (the "aerobic threshold", where lactate first rises
above baseline) and **LT2 / MLSS** (the "anaerobic threshold", the highest
intensity holdable in a metabolic steady state). LT2 is the single best
physiological predictor of endurance race performance — better than VO₂max — and
it is the **most trainable** of the endurance determinants, which makes the pace
and heart rate at threshold the central dial the coach turns. The lab gold
standard is a blood-lactate step test; in the field we estimate it from a sustained
~30-minute time trial or critical-speed math, accepting a real error band
(commonly ±5 bpm / ±5% pace) and refining it from the runner's own data.

## What it is

When you exercise, muscles produce lactate continuously; at low intensities your
body clears (oxidises and recycles) it just as fast, so blood lactate stays near
rest (~1 mmol/L). As intensity rises you cross two landmarks:

- **LT1 (aerobic threshold / first lactate threshold):** the lowest intensity at
  which blood lactate rises *measurably* above baseline (commonly fixed near
  ~2 mmol/L, or detected as the first inflection of the lactate curve). This is
  roughly the upper edge of comfortable "easy" running and the dividing line
  between the easy and moderate domains. It is the intensity the bulk of base/Zone-2
  mileage targets.
- **LT2 (anaerobic threshold / second lactate threshold):** the highest intensity
  at which lactate production and clearance still balance over time. Above it,
  blood lactate climbs relentlessly until you must slow. LT2 corresponds closely
  to the **Maximal Lactate Steady State (MLSS)** — the practical, physiological
  definition of "threshold" — and to a fixed ~4 mmol/L marker historically called
  OBLA (Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation).
- **MLSS:** the highest *constant* workload at which blood lactate stays stable
  (rises ≤1 mmol/L) across the back half of a long steady effort. It is the
  reference standard the others approximate.

**Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR)** is simply the heart rate observed at LT2.
In trained runners it typically sits at **~85–92% of HRmax** (the looser "80–90%"
band covers less-trained runners at the low end and elites at the high end).
Threshold *pace* in a fit recreational runner is close to the pace they could race
for ~60 minutes (roughly 15K–half-marathon effort).

Typical ranges for velocity at LT2 (treadmill/road, well-rested):

| Population | LT2 as %VO₂max | LT2 as %HRmax |
|---|---|---|
| Untrained | ~50–60% | ~75–80% |
| Recreational trained | ~70–80% | ~82–88% |
| Elite endurance | ~85–90% | ~88–92% |

## Physiology / mechanism

Lactate is not a waste product or the cause of fatigue — it is a fuel and a
shuttle. At rest and easy intensities, lactate generated by glycolysis is taken
up and oxidised by mitochondria (in the same fibre, neighbouring slow-twitch
fibres, the heart, and liver). Blood lactate concentration reflects the **balance**
between appearance (production, biased toward fast-twitch fibre recruitment and
glycolytic flux) and disappearance (clearance, driven by mitochondrial density and
the MCT lactate-transporter network).

- **Below LT1:** energy is overwhelmingly aerobic, fat oxidation contributes
  meaningfully, and lactate is cleared as fast as it appears — net blood lactate
  flat.
- **LT1 → LT2 ("heavy" domain):** more fast-twitch fibres recruited, glycolytic
  flux up, lactate appearance exceeds resting clearance so blood lactate settles
  at a new, *elevated but stable* plateau. The runner is still in a metabolic
  steady state but with rising strain.
- **At LT2 / MLSS:** the highest intensity where appearance and clearance still
  reach equilibrium. VO₂, heart rate and lactate can hold a (drifting) plateau.
- **Above LT2 ("severe" domain):** appearance outstrips maximal clearance; lactate,
  VO₂ and ventilation rise inexorably (the VO₂ slow component drives VO₂ toward
  max), and time to exhaustion collapses to minutes.

Training raises threshold chiefly by **improving clearance and reducing
production at a given pace** — more mitochondria, more capillaries, more MCT1
transporters, greater fat oxidation — so a higher absolute speed can be sustained
before lactate runs away. Because these adaptations respond strongly to volume of
aerobic and threshold work, threshold velocity is the most *plastic* of the
endurance traits.

## The evidence

- **[Established]** Endurance performance is determined by the interaction of three
  traits — VO₂max (the ceiling), lactate threshold (the usable fraction of that
  ceiling), and running economy (the cost of a given pace) — not by VO₂max alone
  [Coyle 1995]. Foundational data: in 18 male distance runners, the velocity at
  onset of plasma lactate accumulation was significantly related to performance at
  every distance from 3.2 km to the marathon, and tracked performance better than
  VO₂max alone [Farrell et al. 1979].

- **[Established]** LT1 and LT2 are real, physiologically distinct landmarks, but
  the **terminology and the exact detection method are inconsistent across the
  literature** — dozens of named thresholds (LT, OBLA, IAT, Dmax, MLSS, etc.) and
  fixed cut-offs (2 and 4 mmol/L) coexist, which is why threshold numbers from
  different labs/methods are not interchangeable [Faude et al. 2009].

- **[Probable]** MLSS is a reproducible *intensity*: in a single cycling
  test–retest study (n = 32), the **power and heart rate at MLSS were highly
  reliable** (CV ≈ 3% for power, ≈ 6.3% for HR; ICC 0.98 and 0.92), while the
  **blood-lactate concentration at MLSS itself was much noisier** (CV ≈ 16.6%,
  ICC 0.71) — i.e. the *workload* is stable day to day, but the absolute lactate
  value is not [Hauser et al. 2013]. (Single-study reliability data in cyclists;
  direction is corroborated by the broader MLSS literature but the exact
  coefficients should not be treated as settled constants.)

- **[Established]** The historical fixed **4 mmol/L** "anaerobic threshold" was an
  *average* approximation of MLSS derived by Mader, Heck and colleagues in the late
  1970s–80s, never a universal constant. Individual MLSS lactate varies widely
  (commonly ~3–7 mmol/L), so prescribing off a fixed 4 mmol/L value misclassifies
  many athletes [Heck & Wackerhage 2024; Faude et al. 2009].

- **[Probable]** In trained runners, LT2 sits at roughly **88% of HRmax** and **79%
  of VO₂max** when first measured in a step test — but during a sustained run at
  that intensity, HR drifts upward (~+4% of HRmax over 40 min) due to cardiac
  drift, while lactate creeps from ~2 to ~2.85 mmol/L. Individual responses vary
  considerably (HRmax% range ~84–91% at detection) [Fleckenstein et al. 2023].

- **[Probable]** **Critical Speed/Power (CS/CP)** — derived purely from the
  speed–time relationship of several maximal efforts — is a close cousin of MLSS
  and a strong threshold surrogate, but it is **not identical**: in well-trained
  runners CS averaged ~8% *higher* than MLSS (16.4 vs 15.2 km/h), and the authors
  argue CS better marks the true upper boundary of the steady-state domain
  [Nixon et al. 2021]. With strict, matched methodology the CP–MLSS gap can be
  narrowed to a few watts, so much of the historical discrepancy is methodological
  [Caen et al. 2024].

- **[Probable]** A single-session blood-lactate marker, the **minimum lactate
  equivalent (LEmin, an LT1-type marker)**, predicted velocity at MLSS in trained
  runners with r = 0.91 (83% of variance), bias ~0.00 km/h and limits of agreement
  ±0.89 km/h (±5.9%) — i.e. even a good lab surrogate carries a ~6% error band
  [Garcia-Tabar & Gorostiaga 2018].

- **[Emerging / Contested]** **Wearable estimates of LTHR/threshold pace are
  convenient but imprecise.** Across three smartwatch brands, LTHR estimates showed
  no significant mean difference vs lab but a mean absolute error of ~9–11 bpm
  (MAPE ~6–7%) and weak per-watch correlations (r 0.13–0.67); threshold *pace* was
  systematically *over*estimated (MAPE 13–26%). They should not be the sole basis
  for intensity prescription [Lu et al. 2025].

- **[Established]** Lactate threshold (as velocity at LT2) is **highly trainable**
  and improvements in it track improvements in performance better than changes in
  VO₂max, which plateaus relatively early in trained athletes [Coyle 1995;
  Faude et al. 2009].

## How we compute it

**Lab gold standard (ground truth).** Incremental step test (3–5 min stages) with
capillary blood lactate sampled each stage; the lactate–intensity curve yields LT1
(first rise / Dmax-modified / LEmin) and LT2 (e.g. 4 mmol/L fixed, or +1.0–1.5
mmol/L over LT1, or modified-Dmax). True **MLSS** requires several constant-load
days at 3–5 W or ~0.5 km/h increments, sampling lactate every 5 min over 30 min;
MLSS = highest load where ΔLactate(10→30 min) ≤ 1 mmol/L. This is the reference,
but it is invasive, equipment-heavy, and multi-session.

**Field estimates (what `@daud/core` actually uses).**

1. **30-minute time-trial LTHR (primary field method, practitioner standard /
   Friel protocol).** Run an all-out, evenly-paced solo 30 min on flat terrain;
   **LTHR = average HR of the final 20 minutes**; threshold pace ≈ average pace of
   the final 20 min. Tends to *slightly over*estimate true MLSS for many runners,
   so apply a small downward correction (~3–5%) for zone-setting.
   `@daud/core: lactateThresholdHr` (not yet computed — pending test ingestion).
2. **Critical Speed.** Fit 2–3 maximal efforts of different durations
   (e.g. 3, 6/9, 12 min, or recent race results from ~3–20 min) to
   `distance = CS·time + D′`; CS is the asymptote. CS is a good LT2/threshold
   surrogate but runs a few % *above* MLSS — treat it as an upper-bound estimate.
   `@daud/core: criticalSpeed`.
3. **Race-derived.** Recent 10K–half-marathon race pace/HR is a practical LT2
   anchor for trained runners.

**Error to surface to the coach:** field LTHR carries roughly **±5 bpm**; field/CS
threshold pace roughly **±5%** vs lab; wearable auto-estimates worse still (HR
~±9–11 bpm, pace systematically fast). Always treat any single estimate as a prior
to be updated from the runner's logged sessions.

## How the coach uses it

Threshold is the anchor for **pace/HR zones, workout prescription, and progress
tracking**. Core logic:

- **Easy/base running lives below LT1** (≈ ≤ ~80% HRmax / conversational). Most
  weekly volume sits here; this is where aerobic adaptations that raise threshold
  are built.
- **Threshold ("tempo") work targets LT2 ± a hair** — sustained or cruise-interval
  efforts at ~LTHR (≈ 85–92% HRmax for trained runners) to push MLSS upward.
- **Above LT2 is the hard/severe domain** — VO₂ intervals; capped in volume and
  by recovery rules.

**By stage:**

- **Stage 1 (beginner):** Do **not** demand a lab or even a 30-min TT early. Anchor
  intensity on RPE + the conversational (talk-test) proxy for LT1, and a coarse
  %HRmax band. Beginners' LT2 may be only ~80–85% HRmax; assuming 88–90% would
  push them too hard. Keep nearly all running easy.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** Run a **30-min TT** (or use a recent 5K–10K race) to set
  LTHR and threshold pace; introduce structured tempo/threshold sessions at that
  pace. Re-test every 6–10 weeks; expect threshold *pace* to improve even when HRmax
  and VO₂max look static.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** Use CS + race data + (ideally) periodic lab lactate to set
  precise zones and pace races as fractions of LTHR (≈ 99–102% LTHR for 10K,
  ~92–97% for half, ~85–92% for marathon — practitioner consensus). Watch for
  threshold velocity rising as the key adaptation signal.

**Cross-checks (mandatory):**
- In **heat, at altitude, when dehydrated, or when fatigued**, HR drifts up and
  decouples from pace — weight RPE and pace alongside LTHR, and expect HR to read
  high relative to true effort.
- Account for **cardiac drift**: HR at a fixed threshold pace rises over a long
  session; do not re-zone off a drifted reading.
- If two estimates disagree (e.g. CS vs 30-min TT), prefer the one corroborated by
  recent race data and flag the uncertainty to the runner.

## Honesty & uncertainty

- **Method-dependence is the biggest trap.** "Lactate threshold" is a family of
  non-interchangeable definitions. A number is meaningless without its method;
  comparing an OBLA-4mmol value to a modified-Dmax value to a watch estimate is
  comparing different things [Faude et al. 2009].
- **The 4 mmol/L "anaerobic threshold" is a population average, not a personal
  truth.** Individual MLSS lactate ranges roughly 3–7 mmol/L; fixed cut-offs
  misclassify many runners [Heck & Wackerhage 2024].
- **Blood lactate value is noisy even when intensity is stable** (CV ~16% at MLSS)
  — assay, sampling site (earlobe vs fingertip), prior carbohydrate intake,
  glycogen status, and ambient temperature all move it [Hauser et al. 2013].
- **Day-to-day and within-run drift:** HR at threshold pace creeps up over a run
  (~+4% HRmax/40 min) and varies with heat, sleep, hydration and stress, so LTHR
  is a band, not a line [Fleckenstein et al. 2023].
- **CS ≠ MLSS.** The "true" boundary of the sustainable domain is genuinely
  debated; CS tends to sit a few % above MLSS, and which one is the "real" maximal
  metabolic steady state is unsettled [Nixon et al. 2021; Caen et al. 2024].
- **Wearable auto-estimates are convenient but can be off by ~9–11 bpm and bias
  pace fast** — useful as a trend, weak as an absolute [Lu et al. 2025].
- **Individualisation beats any default.** Every value here is a starting prior to
  be refined from the runner's own pace–HR–RPE history. When the model's estimate
  conflicts with how a runner consistently performs, trust the data.

## Safety bounds

- Field threshold tests (30-min TT, max-effort CS trials) are **near-maximal
  efforts** — gate behind adequate base fitness, medical clearance where relevant,
  and full recovery; never schedule for an injured, ill, or acutely fatigued
  runner. Stage-1 runners should not perform all-out 30-min TTs.
- Do **not** prescribe sustained training *above* LT2 routinely; the severe domain
  is non-steady-state and accumulates fatigue fast — keep it a small, recovery-gated
  fraction of the week.
- LTHR is **invalid as a hard ceiling in heat/altitude/dehydration** — defer to RPE
  and pace; do not push a runner to hit an LTHR number when HR is environmentally
  inflated.
- These are mirrored as guardrails in `@daud/core` intensity/recovery checks.

## Bottom line

- **Act on confidently (conclusive):**
  - LT1 and LT2/MLSS are **real, physiologically distinct** intensity landmarks, and
    velocity at LT2 is the **single best physiological predictor of endurance race
    performance** — better than VO₂max alone, and the **most trainable** of the
    endurance determinants [Coyle 1995; Farrell et al. 1979; Faude et al. 2009].
  - Anchor zones to LT2/LTHR, keep most volume **below LT1**, and track **threshold
    velocity** as the headline adaptation signal.
  - In trained runners LTHR sits at roughly **85–92% of HRmax** (the wider 80–90%
    band spans less-trained to elite).
  - A **fixed 4 mmol/L lactate is a population average, not a personal threshold** —
    never prescribe off it; individual MLSS lactate ranges ~3–7 mmol/L
    [Heck & Wackerhage 2024; Faude et al. 2009].
  - **Every field/wearable estimate carries a real error band** (field LTHR ≈ ±5 bpm,
    field/CS pace ≈ ±5%, wearable worse) and must be treated as a prior to refine from
    the runner's own data [Garcia-Tabar & Gorostiaga 2018; Lu et al. 2025].
  - Environmentally inflated or drifted HR (heat/altitude/dehydration/fatigue) makes
    LTHR an invalid hard ceiling — defer to RPE + pace.

- **Hold loosely (unsettled):**
  - The **exact equivalence of Critical Speed and MLSS**: CS sits a few % above MLSS
    and which one is the "true" maximal metabolic steady state is genuinely debated;
    the gap shrinks with matched methodology [Nixon et al. 2021; Caen et al. 2024].
  - The **precise LTHR %HRmax for a given individual** — it varies with training
    status, drifts within a run, and is a band, not a line [Fleckenstein et al. 2023].
  - **Wearable auto-estimates** as absolute values — convenient trend tools, but pace
    is systematically biased fast and per-device accuracy is inconsistent
    [Lu et al. 2025].
  - The **exact size of the downward correction** to apply to a 30-min-TT LTHR for
    zone-setting (practitioner ~3–5%) — protocol is practitioner standard, not
    RCT-validated.

## Coach Directives

- **D1:** Treat **LT2/MLSS pace and LTHR as the primary anchor** for zone-setting
  and progress tracking; report improvement in threshold *velocity* as the headline
  endurance-adaptation signal. — confidence: Established
- **D2:** Keep the **majority of weekly volume below LT1** (easy/conversational,
  ≈ ≤80% HRmax); this builds the aerobic base that raises threshold. — confidence:
  Established
- **D3:** Default trained-runner **LTHR to ~85–92% HRmax**; for beginners assume a
  **lower band (~80–88%)** and confirm via RPE/talk-test before trusting a number.
  — confidence: Probable
- **D4:** Estimate LTHR from a **30-min solo TT (avg HR of final 20 min)**; apply a
  ~3–5% downward correction when setting steady zones, since the TT over-estimates
  MLSS. — confidence: Probable (protocol = practitioner standard)
- **D5:** Treat **Critical Speed as an upper-bound LT2 surrogate** (~a few % above
  MLSS), not as MLSS itself; reconcile with race data before prescribing. —
  confidence: Probable
- **D6:** Attach an explicit **uncertainty band to every estimate** — field LTHR
  ±~5 bpm, field/CS pace ±~5%, wearable auto-estimates worse (HR ±~9–11 bpm, pace
  biased fast) — and refine the prior from the runner's logged pace–HR–RPE. —
  confidence: Established
- **D7:** **Never treat a fixed 4 mmol/L lactate** (or any single blood-lactate
  value) as the runner's threshold; individual MLSS lactate varies widely. —
  confidence: Established
- **D8:** When HR is environmentally inflated (**heat, altitude, dehydration,
  fatigue**) or has drifted within a long run, **down-weight LTHR and prescribe by
  RPE + pace**. — confidence: Established (safety-critical)
- **D9:** Gate all **near-maximal threshold tests** behind base fitness and full
  recovery; do not test injured/ill/fatigued or Stage-1 runners. — confidence:
  Established (safety-critical)
- **D10:** Keep **above-LT2 (severe-domain) work a small, recovery-gated fraction**
  of the week; do not prescribe it as steady-state training. — confidence:
  Established
