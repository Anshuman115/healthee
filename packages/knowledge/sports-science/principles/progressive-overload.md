# Progressive Overload & Adaptation

## Summary

Fitness improves when the body is exposed to a training stress slightly greater
than it is used to, then allowed to recover and adapt — **progressive overload**.
The directional principle (stress → recovery → adaptation; too much too soon
injures) is **Established** and underpins all endurance training. What is **far
less settled is the dose**: the popular **"increase no more than 10% per week"
rule has weak and largely negative evidence** — a randomized controlled trial
found it did not reduce novice-runner injuries [Buist 2008] and a 36-study
systematic review concluded it "is not justified" [Fredette 2022]. The strongest
recent signal points elsewhere: a 5,200-runner GPS cohort found injury risk is
driven by **single-session distance spikes** (a run >10% longer than your longest
of the past 30 days), not gradual weekly totals [Frandsen 2025]. So: overload
deliberately and patiently, cap *single-run* jumps tightly, deload periodically,
push into short **functional overreaching** only with care, and watch for the
warning signs of **non-functional overreaching / overtraining** — but treat the
exact numeric ramp rules as soft heuristics, not physical laws.

## What it is

**Progressive overload** is the systematic, gradual increase of training demand
over time so that the body is repeatedly nudged just beyond its current capacity,
adapts, and raises that capacity. In running, the load "knobs" you can turn are:

- **Volume** — weekly distance or time (the primary endurance lever).
- **Frequency** — runs per week.
- **Intensity** — pace / effort / time spent in higher HR or power zones.
- **Density** — how much recovery sits between hard efforts.
- **Specificity / terrain** — long runs, hills, race-pace work, surface.

The craft is changing **one variable at a time, in small steps**, and not
stacking volume *and* intensity *and* frequency increases in the same week.

**Supercompensation** is the classic textbook model that motivates overload: a
hard session temporarily *lowers* performance capacity (fatigue), and with
adequate recovery the body rebuilds to a level slightly *above* baseline. Time
the next stimulus to land on that elevated point and fitness ratchets upward;
mistime it (too soon → no recovery; too late → adaptation fades) and you stall.

**Deload / down weeks** are planned reductions in load (typically a ~40–50%
volume cut for ~1 week) inserted every few weeks to let accumulated fatigue
dissipate before the next build.

**Overreaching → overtraining** is the spectrum that defines *how far* overload
can go before it backfires [Meeusen 2013]:

| State | What it is | Recovery time | Net effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional overreaching (FOR)** | Deliberate short-term overload; a temporary performance dip | Days to ~1 week | **Positive** — supercompensation, performance rebounds higher |
| **Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)** | Overload beyond recovery capacity; stagnation + fatigue/mood disturbance | Weeks to months | **Negative** — lost training time, no gain |
| **Overtraining syndrome (OTS)** | Severe, prolonged maladaptation | Months to years (sometimes career-altering) | **Harmful** — may never fully recover prior level |

Critically, **NFOR and OTS look identical in the moment** — they can only be
distinguished retrospectively by how long recovery takes [Meeusen 2013].

## Physiology / mechanism

Overload works because training is a **stressor that disturbs homeostasis**, and
the body's defensive response is to rebuild stronger than before — a sport-science
reading of Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (alarm → resistance/adaptation →
exhaustion). The "exhaustion" stage is exactly NFOR/OTS: the same stress that
builds fitness in moderation degrades it in excess.

At the cellular level a single endurance bout triggers signalling cascades
(AMPK, PGC-1α, calcium-calmodulin pathways) that drive **mitochondrial
biogenesis, capillary growth, increased blood and plasma volume, raised stroke
volume, and improved substrate handling**. Structural tissues — tendon, bone,
muscle, fascia — also remodel, but on a **slower timescale than the
cardiovascular/metabolic adaptations**. This mismatch matters: your heart and
mitochondria can often handle a load jump your tendons and bones cannot yet,
which is why running injuries are overwhelmingly **musculoskeletal overuse**
problems triggered by load that outran tissue remodelling.

The best-supported *model* of the overload–recovery dynamic is **not** the neat
single supercompensation curve but the **fitness–fatigue (two-component) model**
[Banister 1975]: every session produces both a fitness impulse and a fatigue
impulse, and **fatigue decays faster than fitness**. Adaptation is what's left
when the fast fatigue component has drained away — which is *why* recovery
(easy days, deloads, tapers) reveals fitness, and why chronically inadequate
recovery (NFOR/OTS) leaves only the accumulating fatigue.

The **training-injury prevention paradox** completes the picture: a high
*chronic* load, built up gradually, is **protective** — it develops the very
tissue and aerobic robustness that lets a runner absorb future load — whereas the
danger is a high *acute* spike on top of a low chronic base [Gabbett 2016]. The
goal of progressive overload is therefore to grow the chronic platform steadily
without sharp acute spikes.

## The evidence

Lead claims by strength. The **core principle is Established**; the **specific
numeric prescriptions are weak, mixed, or refuted** — and we say so plainly.

- **[Established]** **Adaptation requires overload plus recovery; chronic
  inadequate recovery causes maladaptation.** This is the foundation of the
  ECSS/ACSM joint consensus statement, synthesising decades of work across
  endurance sports [Meeusen 2013]. The FOR→NFOR→OTS spectrum is broadly accepted;
  short overload followed by recovery improves performance, sustained overload
  without it degrades it. Population: elite and recreational endurance athletes.

- **[Established]** **A high, gradually built chronic training load is protective,
  not inherently dangerous — "too much, too soon" is the real risk, not "too
  much."** The training-injury prevention paradox, drawn from large team-sport
  cohorts, shows athletes with higher well-built chronic loads sustain *fewer*
  injuries than under-trained athletes; the harm comes from acute load spiking
  far above the chronic base [Gabbett 2016]. Directionally consistent with the
  running-specific spike findings below.

- **[Probable — strong but single, unreplicated cohort]** **In running, injury risk
  is driven by single-session distance spikes, not gradual weekly increases.** The
  Garmin-RUNSAFE study — a prospective cohort of **5,205 runners over 588,071
  sessions** (1,820 sustained an injury) — found a dose-response: a single run
  **>10% longer than the longest run of the prior 30 days** raised overuse-injury
  rate (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.31–2.05), with **>30%** HR 1.52 (95% CI 1.16–2.00) and
  **>100% (more than doubling)** HR 2.28 (95% CI 1.50–3.48). Notably, **the
  week-to-week mileage ratio showed no association, and ACWR showed an *inverse*
  dose-response** (higher ACWR went with *lower*, not higher, injury risk —
  opposite to the ACWR "sweet-spot" theory) [Frandsen 2025]. This is the single
  strongest, most objective running-specific evidence we have on load progression,
  and it reframes the target from weekly totals to **single-run jumps** — but it is
  one large *observational* cohort (association, not proven causation) and not yet
  replicated, so we grade it **Probable**, not Established.

- **[Myth / Refuted as a protective rule]** **The "10% per week" rule does not
  demonstrably prevent injury.** The GRONORUN **RCT** randomised 532 novice
  runners to a 13-week graded program built on the 10% rule vs a standard 8-week
  program; injury incidence was **essentially identical (20.8% vs 20.3%)**
  [Buist 2008]. A systematic review of **36 studies (33 cohorts + 3 RCTs, 23,047
  runners, mostly high quality)** concluded the 10% rule "is not justified" and
  that **no specific progression recommendation can be issued** because the
  evidence is conflicting (5 studies linked rapid distance increase to injury, 5
  found no association) [Fredette 2022]. The 10% rule is a reasonable *conservative
  default* for beginners, but its status as an evidence-based injury-prevention law
  is a myth.

- **[Probable]** **Where a weekly-progression danger threshold does show up, it is
  nearer ~30%, not 10%.** A 1-year prospective cohort of 874 novice runners found
  that those who increased weekly distance by **>30% over 2 weeks** were more prone
  to "distance-related" overuse injuries (patellofemoral pain, ITB syndrome,
  medial tibial stress syndrome, patellar tendinopathy, gluteus-medius injury)
  than those who progressed **<10%** [Nielsen 2014]. So a *large* sudden jump does
  appear to matter — but the data point to tens-of-percent spikes, not the precise
  10% line. Findings across the literature remain **conflicting** [Fredette 2022].

- **[Established as a directional model / Emerging as a precise schedule]**
  **Supercompensation.** The directional claim — recover after overload and you
  adapt above baseline — is sound and maps onto the well-supported fitness–fatigue
  model [Banister 1975]. But the tidy *single curve* with a predictable, schedulable
  "supercompensation window" is an **oversimplification**: different qualities
  (glycogen, enzymes, tendon, mitochondria) recover on very different timescales,
  so there is no one curve to time, and the precise window is highly individual and
  not cleanly demonstrated in humans. Use it as a teaching heuristic, not a
  stopwatch.

- **[Emerging / practitioner consensus]** **Deload weeks.** Deloading is near-
  universal in coaching practice and documented in coach interviews (reduced
  volume/intensity programmed every ~4–6 weeks for ~5–7 days) [Bell 2023], but **direct
  experimental evidence is thin and not clearly favourable.** The best controlled
  test — a 9-week **RCT** in 39 resistance-trained adults — found a mid-program
  1-week deload produced **no hypertrophy benefit and slightly *less* strength**
  than training straight through [Coleman 2024]. That is resistance training, not
  running, and short-term; it does not refute deloads for managing endurance
  fatigue/injury (their main rationale), but it does mean the "deloads boost
  adaptation" claim is unproven. Treat deloads as **prudent fatigue/injury
  management**, not a proven performance enhancer.

- **[Contested]** **ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio) as an injury predictor.**
  A 2025 meta-analysis of 22 cohort studies found a statistically significant
  association between ACWR and injury (ES 0.72) and an apparent lower-injury
  "sweet spot" of **0.8–1.3** [Qin 2025], echoing Gabbett's original guidance
  [Gabbett 2016]. **But the metric is heavily criticised**: Impellizzeri and
  colleagues detail conceptual and statistical pitfalls — ratio-data artefacts,
  no causal framework, arbitrary time windows, and inflation of the acute term
  without added predictive value [Impellizzeri 2020] — and in the running-specific
  Garmin cohort ACWR did **not** predict injury in the expected direction (if
  anything an inverse dose-response) [Frandsen 2025]. Use ACWR only as a **soft
  monitoring signal**, never a hard gate.

- **[Established]** **OTS is real, serious, and worth preventing.** OTS is most
  common in endurance sport; reviews estimate a substantial lifetime prevalence in
  endurance athletes and note it can cause **months-to-years** of underperformance,
  occasionally career-ending [Meeusen 2013]. Prevalence figures vary widely by
  definition and are imprecise, but the clinical reality and the prevention
  imperative are not in doubt.

## How we compute it

Progressive overload is a **planning + monitoring** layer, not a single number.
It leans on the load metrics Daud already tracks:

- **Weekly volume trend** — the primary progression signal. Track week-over-week
  change, but weight **single-session distance vs the 30-day longest run** at
  least as heavily (per [Frandsen 2025]).
- **Single-run spike check (new, high-priority):** for each planned/recorded run,
  compute `runDistance / max(longestRun, last 30 days)`. Flag at **>1.10**, warn
  hard at **>1.30**, and treat **>2.0 (doubling)** as a near-hard stop. This is the
  best-evidenced load guardrail we have for running.
- **CTL / ATL / TSB** — chronic load ≈ fitness platform, acute load ≈ fatigue,
  TSB = CTL − ATL ≈ form. Progressive overload should grow **CTL** steadily;
  a sustained deeply negative TSB without rebound is the computational fingerprint
  of drifting toward NFOR.
- **ACWR / weekly-load ratio** — computed and shown as a **soft** flag only
  (rough 0.8–1.3 guide), explicitly *not* a gate, given its contested status
  [Impellizzeri 2020; Frandsen 2025].

Implementation notes:

- **Default ramp:** progress weekly volume **conservatively (~5–10%/wk as a
  starting heuristic)** for most runners, but treat this as a soft default, not a
  safety law — the harder bound is on single-run spikes.
- **Deload cadence:** insert a ~40–50% volume down-week roughly **every 3–5 weeks**
  of progressive loading (individualised); not yet auto-scheduled.
- **One knob at a time:** the engine should avoid simultaneously increasing volume
  and intensity in the same microcycle.
- All thresholds are **starting estimates to be overwritten by the individual's
  measured response and injury history** (see `individualization`).

## How the coach uses it

**Progression logic by stage:**

- **Stage 1 (beginner).** Tissue robustness is the binding constraint. Prioritise
  **frequency and consistency over volume jumps**; keep single-run increases
  small; use run/walk and easy effort. A conservative ~10%/week volume default is
  fine *as caution*, but the coach should be clear it is prudence, not a guarantee.
  Deload more often (every ~3–4 weeks). Never stack volume + intensity.

- **Stage 2 (developing).** Deliberate, structured overload: build volume in
  3–5 week blocks separated by down-weeks, then begin layering intensity once a
  durable aerobic base exists. Introduce the **long-run spike guardrail** as a
  hard habit. Begin monitoring TSB/fatigue trends for early NFOR signals.

- **Stage 3 (racing/competitive).** Full periodised overload (see `periodization`),
  including **deliberate short functional-overreaching blocks** (≤~2 weeks of
  elevated load) followed by recovery/taper — but only for experienced,
  well-monitored athletes, never beginners. Tight surveillance for NFOR/OTS.

**Decision rules the coach applies:**

- **Cap single-run jumps:** do not prescribe a run >~10% longer than the runner's
  longest of the past 30 days without good reason; never schedule a sudden
  doubling of longest distance [Frandsen 2025].
- **Spread, don't spike:** to add weekly volume, prefer adding an easy run or
  lengthening several runs slightly over inflating one long run.
- **Recover to adapt:** after a hard overload, schedule adequate easy/recovery
  before the next quality stimulus; don't chase fitness by stacking hard days
  (fitness–fatigue logic).
- **Deload on schedule and on signal:** insert planned down-weeks; also deload
  *reactively* when fatigue, sleep, mood, or resting HR trend the wrong way.
- **Escalate on NFOR signs:** unexplained performance decline + persistent fatigue
  / mood disturbance / disturbed sleep / elevated resting HR over **weeks** → cut
  load and prioritise recovery; do not "train through" it [Meeusen 2013].

## Honesty & uncertainty

- **The 10% rule is folklore dressed as science.** It is a fine conservative
  default but it failed its one good RCT [Buist 2008] and was rejected by a large
  systematic review [Fredette 2022]. The coach must **not** present it as proven
  injury prevention.
- **The literature on weekly progression is genuinely conflicting.** Roughly half
  of studies link rapid weekly increases to injury and half don't [Fredette 2022];
  even the supportive cohort points to a ~30% threshold, not 10% [Nielsen 2014].
  This is real uncertainty, not a settled rule we can quote a number from.
- **The single-session-spike finding is strong but young.** [Frandsen 2025] is one
  large, well-designed cohort, not yet a replicated body of work; it is
  observational (association, not proven causation) and its population skews male,
  middle-aged, and self-selected Garmin users. We lead with it because it is the
  best running-specific evidence available, while flagging it needs replication.
- **Supercompensation is a heuristic, not a schedule.** There is no single curve to
  time; recovery rates differ by tissue and individual. Avoid promising a precise
  "window."
- **Deload benefit is unproven.** Deloads are sensible fatigue/injury management
  and near-universal in practice, but the best controlled trial showed no
  adaptation benefit (and slightly less strength) vs training through [Coleman
  2024]. We keep deloads for **risk management**, not because they are shown to
  boost fitness.
- **ACWR is contested.** It has known statistical and conceptual flaws
  [Impellizzeri 2020] and failed to predict running injury in the largest relevant
  cohort [Frandsen 2025]. Use it lightly; never let it override the runner's own
  signals.
- **NFOR vs OTS is only knowable in hindsight.** At the time, an athlete can't tell
  a recoverable overreach from the start of a months-long syndrome [Meeusen 2013],
  so the coach must err toward early load reduction rather than waiting for
  certainty.
- **Individual variation dominates.** Tissue tolerance, training history, age,
  sleep, nutrition, life stress, and genetics shift every threshold here by large
  margins. Population numbers are priors; the runner's own history is the truth
  (see `individualization`).

## Safety bounds

- **Single-run distance spike (highest-priority running guardrail):** avoid
  prescribing a run **>~10%** longer than the longest run of the prior 30 days;
  treat **>~30%** as a warning requiring justification; treat a **>~100% jump
  (doubling longest distance)** as a near-hard stop — overuse-injury rate roughly
  doubles there [Frandsen 2025]. This bound is mirrored as a guardrail in
  `@daud/core`.
- **No simultaneous spikes:** never increase volume *and* intensity *and*
  frequency in the same microcycle.
- **Mandatory deloads:** do not run progressive overload indefinitely without a
  down-week; insert one at least every ~5 weeks of loading (more often for
  beginners) — for fatigue/injury risk management.
- **NFOR/OTS stop condition:** on a multi-week pattern of unexplained
  underperformance plus fatigue/mood/sleep/resting-HR disturbance, **reduce load
  and recover — do not progress** [Meeusen 2013].
- **Functional-overreaching blocks are experienced-athlete-only**, time-boxed
  (≤~2 weeks), monitored, and always followed by recovery; never applied to
  Stage 1 runners.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**

- Adaptation needs overload **and** recovery; chronic under-recovery causes
  maladaptation (NFOR/OTS). [Meeusen 2013]
- Build the **chronic** load gradually — a high, well-built base is protective;
  acute spikes on a low base are the danger. [Gabbett 2016]
- **Cap large single-run distance jumps** (be cautious past ~10–30% over the
  30-day longest; never double it) — a prudent, low-cost guardrail and the
  best-evidenced running-specific injury signal. We act on it confidently *because
  it is conservative and cheap*, while noting the precise numbers rest on a single
  (large, strong) observational cohort, not yet replicated. [Frandsen 2025]
- Change **one load variable at a time**; don't stack volume + intensity.
- OTS is real and serious; **stop and recover** at the first multi-week signs of
  non-functional overreaching. [Meeusen 2013]

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**

- The **"10% per week" rule** — conservative default at best, refuted as a
  protective law [Buist 2008; Fredette 2022]; any weekly danger threshold is
  nearer ~30% and the evidence is conflicting [Nielsen 2014].
- **Supercompensation timing** — directional truth, but no precise schedulable
  window.
- **Deload weeks** — sensible fatigue management, but unproven for boosting
  adaptation [Coleman 2024]; cadence (~3–5 wk) is practitioner consensus.
- **ACWR / the 0.8–1.3 "sweet spot"** — contested, statistically flawed, and a
  non-predictor in the largest running cohort [Impellizzeri 2020; Frandsen 2025].

## Coach Directives

- **D1:** Apply progressive overload — increase training demand **gradually and
  systematically, one variable at a time**; never raise volume, intensity and
  frequency together in the same microcycle. — confidence: Established
- **D2:** **Cap single-run distance:** do not prescribe a run more than **~10%
  longer than the runner's longest run in the prior 30 days**; warn at **>30%**;
  treat a **doubling (>100%)** as a near-hard stop. Mirror as a `@daud/core`
  guardrail. — confidence: Probable (one strong, large, unreplicated observational
  cohort). The directive is firm despite Probable evidence only because it is a
  conservative, low-cost safety guardrail, not because the evidence is conclusive.
- **D3:** Treat **"10% per week" weekly-volume progression as a soft beginner
  default, not an injury-prevention law**; do not claim it prevents injury. —
  confidence: Myth/Refuted (as a protective rule)
- **D4:** Grow the **chronic load steadily** so high training loads become
  protective; avoid acute load spiking far above the chronic base. — confidence:
  Established
- **D5:** Insert a **deload/down-week (~40–50% volume cut, ~1 week) every ~3–5
  weeks** of progressive loading (more often for beginners), for fatigue/injury
  management — without claiming it boosts adaptation. — confidence: Emerging /
  practitioner consensus
- **D6:** Sequence **overload → recovery → next stimulus**; after a hard session
  allow adequate easy/recovery before the next quality effort (fitness–fatigue).
  — confidence: Established
- **D7:** Permit **deliberate functional-overreaching blocks only for experienced,
  monitored athletes**, time-boxed to ≤~2 weeks and always followed by recovery;
  never for Stage 1. — confidence: Probable
- **D8:** **Surveil for NFOR/OTS:** on a multi-week pattern of unexplained
  performance decline + fatigue/mood/sleep/resting-HR disturbance, **reduce load
  and recover — do not progress.** — confidence: Established (syndrome);
  Emerging (specific early markers)
- **D9:** Use **ACWR / weekly-load ratios as a soft monitoring flag only** (loose
  0.8–1.3 guide); never gate decisions on it or override the runner's own signals.
  — confidence: Contested
- **D10:** Treat **supercompensation as a directional heuristic**, not a precise
  schedulable window — recover to adapt, but don't promise an exact timing. —
  confidence: Established (direction) / Emerging (precise timing)
- **D11:** **Individualise every threshold** in this doc from the runner's own
  injury history and measured response; population numbers are priors, not gospel.
  — confidence: Established
