# Polarized & Intensity-Distribution Training

## Summary
Training intensity distribution (TID) describes how a runner's total training is
split across easy, moderate, and hard intensities. The most robust finding in
endurance science is that successful athletes do the large majority of their
volume **easy** (~80%) and a minority **hard** — they do *not* live in the
moderate "no man's land." Whether the hard minority is best done as **polarized**
(hard work mostly above threshold, almost nothing in the middle) or **pyramidal**
(a tapering amount of threshold work under a large easy base) is genuinely
**contested**: controlled trials lean slightly toward polarized for raising
VO₂max in already-fit athletes, while observational data on the fastest real-world
marathoners lean pyramidal. The settled coaching takeaway is the **80/20
principle and avoiding the moderate-intensity trap**; the exact shape of the hard
20% is a tunable, individual, event-specific choice — not a law.

## What it is
TID is usually expressed in a **three-zone model** anchored to the two
physiological turn-points (Seiler 2010):

- **Zone 1 (low / easy):** below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold (LT1/VT1)
  — conversational, < ~2 mmol·L⁻¹ lactate. The aerobic base zone.
- **Zone 2 (moderate / threshold):** between LT1 and LT2 — "comfortably hard,"
  ~2–4 mmol·L⁻¹. This is the zone Seiler nicknamed the **"black hole"** or
  **"no man's land."**
- **Zone 3 (high / hard):** above LT2/VT2 / maximal lactate steady state — interval
  and race-specific intensity, typically ≥ ~90% VO₂max in hard efforts.

The named distributions:

| Model | Z1 (easy) | Z2 (threshold) | Z3 (hard) | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polarized (POL)** | ~75–80% | very low (~5%) | ~15–20% | easy + hard, hollow middle |
| **Pyramidal (PYR)** | ~75–80% | moderate (~15%) | small (~5%) | descending Z1>Z2>Z3 |
| **Threshold (THR)** | ~45% | high (~35–55%) | low | middle-heavy |
| **High-volume (HVT)** | ~80–95% | low | ~0–1% | almost all easy |

Note a subtlety that causes endless confusion: **both POL and PYR share the same
~80% easy base.** They differ only in how the remaining ~20% is split between the
middle and the top. "80/20" by itself does *not* specify polarized — a pyramid is
also ~80/20. TID also depends on **how you measure it** (time-in-zone vs
session-goal vs HR vs pace): the same training can look polarized by session
count and pyramidal by time-in-zone.

Typical ranges: recreational runners drift toward too much Z2 (the trap); trained
and elite endurance athletes converge on ~80% Z1 regardless of model.

## Physiology / mechanism
Why an easy-heavy, polarized-or-pyramidal distribution works:

- **Low-intensity, high-volume work** maximizes the *aerobic* adaptive stimulus
  per unit of stress: mitochondrial biogenesis, capillary density, fat oxidation,
  stroke volume / plasma volume expansion, and Type I fiber development — driven
  by signaling (e.g. PGC-1α via calcium/AMPK pathways) that responds strongly to
  **duration**. It does this at low autonomic and structural cost, so large
  volumes are sustainable.
- **High-intensity work** recruits Type II fibers and drives a *different,
  complementary* signaling pathway (greater PGC-1α activation per minute,
  cardiac stretch at high stroke volume, VO₂max stimulus), but at high
  sympathetic and mechanical cost — only a small dose is tolerable before it
  compromises recovery.
- **The moderate "black hole"** is hypothesized to be the worst trade: hard enough
  to incur meaningful sympathetic stress, glycogen depletion, and fatigue, but not
  hard enough to maximally stimulate the top-end adaptations — and it crowds out
  the easy volume that builds the base. You pay a high recovery cost for a blunted
  stimulus. This is the mechanistic case against "junk miles."
- The combination — lots of low-cost aerobic volume plus a few high-value hard
  sessions — is argued to **optimize the ratio of adaptive signal to fatigue
  cost** (Seiler 2010). This is a coherent, well-supported framework, though the
  precise molecular accounting is still partly inferential.

## The evidence

Lead with the strongest evidence. The literature is unusually rich for a training
question, but it is dominated by **small, short RCTs** plus large **observational**
datasets, so confidence is moderate, not absolute.

- **[Established] Elite and well-trained endurance athletes do ~80% of training
  easy (Z1) and a minority hard — across nearly every endurance sport.** This is
  the most replicated finding in the field. *Synthesis of observational/descriptive
  data* across rowing, cycling, running, XC skiing, triathlon, swimming
  [Seiler 2010; Seiler & Kjerland 2006; Stöggl & Sperlich 2015]. The ~80% easy
  share is remarkably consistent; it is the *foundation* the coach can stand on.
  The **2024 marathon dataset of 119,452 runners** found Z1 volume had the
  strongest (negative) correlation with finish time (R² ≥ 0.90) — the faster the
  group, the *more* easy volume they did [Muñiz-Pumares et al. 2024].

- **[Probable] Avoid the moderate-intensity "black hole." Diverting easy volume
  into threshold work tends to *reduce* gains.** The direct causal evidence is a
  *single small controlled trial* in 12 subelite runners over 5 months: a
  low-intensity-emphasis group (~80/12/8) improved cross-country race time ~7% more
  than a between-thresholds group (~67/25/8) [Esteve-Lanao et al. 2007]. That n=12
  trial alone cannot establish the claim, but it is *convergent* with the
  recreational RCT below (compliance subgroup) and the large observational gradient.
  Direction is well-supported; the causal effect size is uncertain.

- **[Probable] Polarized training raises VO₂max more than other distributions —
  but mainly in already-trained athletes and over short blocks.** *Strongest
  evidence: systematic review with meta-analysis of 17 studies / 437 athletes*
  [Oliveira, Boppre & Fonseca 2024, Sports Medicine]. POL was superior for VO₂peak
  (SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.01–0.48, p=0.04, I²=0%, rated **high certainty**) — but the
  advantage **disappeared for time-trial, time-to-exhaustion, and velocity/power at
  threshold** (all non-significant). The VO₂max edge was concentrated in
  **interventions < 12 weeks** (SMD 0.40) and in **highly trained / national-level
  athletes** (SMD 0.46); it vanished beyond 12 weeks and in less-trained runners.
  So: a *real but narrow* advantage — physiological (aerobic power) more than
  performance, short-term more than chronic, elite more than developmental.

- **[Probable] In head-to-head RCTs, polarized beats threshold for performance,
  modestly.** *Meta-analysis of 3 RCTs / 65 athletes* [Rosenblat et al. 2019]:
  moderate effect favoring POL over THR on time-trial (ES −0.66, 95% CI −1.17 to
  −0.15). Caveat: only 3 studies, small n, wide CI. The single
  most-cited RCT [Stöggl & Sperlich 2014, n=48 well-trained, 9 weeks] found POL
  produced the largest gains: VO₂peak +11.7%, time-to-exhaustion +17.4%, vs little
  or no improvement in the THR and HVT groups — but it was short and the THR group
  arguably did an unusually middle-heavy plan.

- **[Probable] In recreational runners POL and threshold both work; POL's edge is
  small and compliance-dependent.** *RCT* in 30 recreational runners, 10 weeks
  [Muñoz et al. 2014]: POL improved 10 km by 5.0% vs 3.5% for between-thresholds —
  the authors concluded there was **not enough evidence to declare a winner**. In
  the *strictly compliant* subgroups the gap widened (7.0% vs 1.6%), hinting the
  benefit depends on actually keeping easy days easy.

- **[Contested] Polarized vs pyramidal: no clear winner; the best evidence
  actually leans pyramidal for distance running.** The fastest real-world
  marathoners are **predominantly pyramidal — > 80% of the fastest group**
  [Muñiz-Pumares et al. 2024, n=119,452]. Elite distance-running reviews find
  pyramidal is the most common pattern, often shifting toward polarized only as
  the race nears [Kenneally et al. 2018; Stöggl & Sperlich 2015]. A direct
  *16-week RCT* in 60 well-trained runners found **periodizing pyramidal→polarized**
  (PYR in base, POL near competition) beat holding either model constant [Filipas
  et al. 2022]. Verdict: the easy-base + small-hard structure matters more than
  whether the hard work sits at threshold or above it; experts genuinely disagree
  on the optimum, and it appears event- and phase-specific.

- **[Emerging] Periodizing the distribution (base = more pyramidal/volume, peak =
  more polarized/specific) may beat any fixed model.** Supported by the Filipas
  2022 RCT and by descriptions of elite practice [Stöggl & Sperlich 2015;
  Kenneally et al. 2018], but prospective trials directly testing *sequencing* are
  few. Promising, not proven. (See `periodization`.)

- **[Myth] "All your easy miles are junk miles."** Refuted. Easy Z1 volume is the
  single strongest correlate of endurance performance in the largest datasets
  [Muñiz-Pumares 2024; Seiler 2010]. "Junk miles" properly names *moderate-intensity
  miles run instead of true easy or true hard* — not easy running itself. Easy
  volume is the base, not filler.

## How we compute it
TID is computed from session data by binning time (or distance, or sessions)
into the three zones. Zone boundaries should be anchored to the runner's own
LT1/LT2 (or VT1/VT2) where known; otherwise estimated from HR or pace anchors:

- **Time-in-zone (HR-based):** % of total training time with HR in each zone.
  Z1 ≈ below ~first ventilatory/lactate threshold (rough HR proxy: < ~80–82% HRmax
  / below the top of the conversational range); Z3 ≈ above LT2 (≈ ≥ ~88–92% HRmax).
  Boundaries are individual — calibrate, don't assume.
- **Session-goal method:** classify each *session* by its primary purpose
  (easy/threshold/interval). Yields a more "polarized-looking" number than
  time-in-zone, because a hard interval session still contains easy warm-up time.
  **Always state which method is used** — the two are not interchangeable.
- Upstream names (the upstream `@daud/core` (a module that exists in no repo — kept as import provenance, not a live dependency)): `intensiteDistribution` / `timeInZone` / `weeklyEasyShare`
  (not all yet computed). HR- and pace-derived zones are *estimates* with real
  error (HR drift in heat, GPS pace noise, individual threshold variation), not
  lab-measured ground truth. Treat the computed easy/hard split as ±5–10%.

## How the coach uses it
The operational core is **protect the easy base and keep hard days genuinely
hard** — the part that is settled — while treating the POL-vs-PYR shape as an
individual, stage-specific dial.

- **The rule that always holds:** aim for **~80% of weekly training time easy
  (Z1)**, with the remaining ~20% split between Z2 and Z3 depending on phase and
  athlete. Police the *easy* days hardest — the most common failure mode in
  recreational runners is easy runs creeping into Z2.
- **Stage 1 (beginner):** the priority is consistency, volume tolerance, and
  durability — keep it overwhelmingly easy (Z1), introduce hard work sparingly
  (1 session/week, often strides or short intervals). Don't agonize over POL vs
  PYR; just keep them out of the black hole and avoid too much hard, too soon.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** establish the 80/20 habit. Base phase can be
  **pyramidal-leaning** (a little threshold under a big easy base) to build
  durability and economy; cap hard sessions at ~2/week. This matches what most
  fast real-world distance runners actually do.
- **Stage 3 (racing / peaking):** shift **toward polarized** as the race nears —
  replace some threshold work with race-specific VO₂max/Z3 intervals while keeping
  the easy base, per the periodization evidence [Filipas 2022]. For 5k–10k and
  shorter, the polarized tilt is better supported; for the marathon, retain more
  threshold/race-pace (more pyramidal), since the event itself lives near LT2.
- **Hard-day cap:** ~2 high-intensity sessions/week for most; a third only with
  strong recovery markers and never at the expense of the easy base. This was
  written as "the **well-supported** ceiling" — it is not: no citation exists for it
  anywhere in this note or that we could find. It is **practitioner convention**
  (#98).
- **Cross-checks:** weight effort/RPE and pace alongside HR (HR drifts in heat and
  with fatigue); if "easy" runs consistently land in Z2 by HR *and* feel hard,
  slow them down regardless of the planned pace.

## Honesty & uncertainty
This topic is a good example of *settled foundation, unsettled details* — the
coach must speak confidently about the base and hedge about the shape.

- **What's solid:** the ~80% easy share and the cost of the moderate-intensity
  trap. These replicate across designs, sports, and decades.
- **What's genuinely unsettled — POL vs PYR:** the controlled trials lean
  polarized (mostly for VO₂max, mostly short-term, mostly in already-fit athletes),
  while the largest observational datasets lean pyramidal for distance running.
  Both can be true: short blocks vs chronic training, and lab markers vs race
  results, may favor different shapes. **The coach should not claim polarized is
  universally optimal.**
- **Small, short studies:** most intervention evidence is n < 60 and ≤ 16 weeks.
  Endurance adaptation plays out over years; we are extrapolating from weeks.
- **Reverse causation in observational data:** elites may do 80% easy *because*
  they run huge volumes that *must* be mostly easy to be survivable — not
  necessarily because 80/20 is causally optimal at lower volumes. The right ratio
  may shift with total volume.
- **Measurement dependence:** "polarized" by session-goal can be "pyramidal" by
  time-in-zone. Many published disagreements are partly definitional. Always
  pin the method.
- **Individual response:** trials report responders and non-responders to the same
  distribution; compliance strongly modulated the effect [Muñoz 2014]. The optimal
  TID is partly genetic/individual and should be refined from the runner's own
  response, not assigned from a population default. (See `individualization`.)
- **Event specificity:** a miler, a 10k runner, and a marathoner have different
  optimal distributions — the marathon lives near threshold, so marathoners
  rationally do more Z2 than the polarized model would prescribe.
- **Volume confound:** the strongest performance correlate in big datasets is
  total easy *volume*, which is entangled with TID. Getting the ratio right matters
  less than accumulating sustainable easy volume in the first place.

## Safety bounds
- **No hard hard-day cap is "safety-critical" per se**, but the operational
  guardrail of **≤ ~3 high-intensity (Z3) sessions/week**, and not increasing hard
  volume and total volume simultaneously, protects against overreaching and
  injury. Mirror the load-progression bounds from `training-load`/`periodization`.
- **The easy base is a safety mechanism, not just a performance one:** keeping
  ~80% genuinely easy limits cumulative high-stress load and is protective against
  injury and non-functional overreaching. Pushing toward threshold-heavy
  distributions raises injury/burnout risk for little or negative return — flag
  this if a runner's data shows Z2/Z3 creeping above ~25–30% of volume for weeks.
- Otherwise: none beyond general load-management bounds.
- **Not enforced in code** — the bounds above are rules for the coach and design
  constraints for the programs builder, not hard guardrails. Only directives a note
  declares `safety_critical` in its frontmatter compile into
  `insights/guard_directives.py`, and this note declares none; D3 is a candidate for that
  mechanism, not a user of it. (D3 carried "(safety-mirrored)" until 2026-08-01, #87.)

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Do roughly **80% of training easy (Z1)**; this is the most replicated finding in
  endurance training and the strongest performance correlate in the largest
  datasets.
- **Avoid the moderate-intensity "black hole"** — don't convert easy volume into
  threshold grinding; "junk miles" are moderate miles, not easy ones.
- **Cap hard sessions at ~2/week** for most runners; keep hard days genuinely hard
  and easy days genuinely easy.
- Easy volume is **the base, not junk** — protect it.

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- **Polarized vs pyramidal** — no universal winner. RCTs lean polarized for VO₂max
  (short-term, trained athletes); the largest observational data lean pyramidal for
  distance running. Individualize and let the runner's response decide.
- **Polarized's performance (not just VO₂max) advantage** is small, inconsistent,
  and fades beyond ~12 weeks and in less-trained runners.
- **Periodizing the distribution** (pyramidal base → polarized peak) is promising
  but rests on limited prospective evidence.
- The **exact easy/threshold/hard split** is event- and athlete-specific, not a
  fixed law.
