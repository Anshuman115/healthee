# Periodization & Tapering

## Summary

Periodization is the deliberate division of training into phases (base → build →
peak → taper) so that fitness accumulates while fatigue is managed, and so that
the runner arrives at a goal race fresh and specifically prepared. The single
best-supported piece is the **taper**: a meta-analysis of 27 studies found that
reducing training **volume by ~41–60% over ~2 weeks while holding intensity and
frequency roughly constant** yields a **~2–3% performance gain** [Bosquet 2007],
replicated by a 2023 endurance-only meta-analysis [Wang 2023]. The *macro*
structure (which periodization model is "best") is far less settled than
practitioners imply: model-to-model differences are small, study quality is low,
and much of the framework is tradition-driven rather than evidence-led [Kiely
2012]. So: taper with confidence, periodize sensibly, but hold the dogma loosely.

## What it is

**Periodization** is the organisation of training over time into nested cycles:

- **Macrocycle** — the whole season or a full race build (≈12–52 weeks).
- **Mesocycle** — a phase with a single dominant focus (≈3–6 weeks).
- **Microcycle** — usually one week; the smallest repeating load/recovery unit.

The classic endurance progression of mesocycles:

| Phase | Primary aim | Typical content | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Aerobic foundation, durability, mitochondrial/capillary density | High volume of easy running, strides, some hills; little race-specific work | 6–16 wk |
| **Build** | Raise threshold and VO₂max; introduce specificity | Tempo/threshold, VO₂max intervals, progressively race-specific long runs | 4–10 wk |
| **Peak / Sharpening** | Convert fitness to race readiness | Race-pace work, sharpening intervals, slight volume trim | 2–4 wk |
| **Taper** | Shed fatigue, preserve fitness | Volume cut at the D1 dose, intensity kept, frequency mostly kept | 1–3 wk |

After the race comes a **transition / off-season** (active recovery, reduced
structure) to dissipate accumulated fatigue and protect long-term motivation.

**Three competing scheduling models** sit on top of these phases:

- **Linear (traditional)** — one quality emphasised at a time, shifting gradually
  from high-volume/low-intensity toward low-volume/high-intensity across the
  macrocycle. The Soviet-origin default [Issurin 2010].
- **Block** — short (≈2–4 wk) concentrated blocks each targeting *few* abilities,
  developed consecutively, exploiting "residual" training effects from the prior
  block [Issurin 2010].
- **Undulating (non-linear)** — intensity and volume oscillate frequently (within
  a week or microcycle) rather than across months. Borrowed largely from strength
  training.

## Physiology / mechanism

Periodization is the practical expression of two interacting models:

1. **Supercompensation / fitness–fatigue.** Each training bout produces *both* a
   fitness impulse and a fatigue impulse; modelled performance ("preparedness")
   is fitness minus fatigue, and **fatigue decays faster than fitness** (Banister
   modelled fatigue decaying ~3× faster) [Banister 1975]. This asymmetry is *why
   tapering works*: cut load and fatigue falls away quickly while the slower-
   decaying fitness lingers, so net preparedness rises for a window of days to a
   couple of weeks.

2. **Progressive overload + specificity (SAID).** Adaptation is specific to the
   stimulus. Aerobic/structural adaptations (mitochondrial density, capillarity,
   stroke volume, tendon and bone durability) accrue slowly and underpin
   everything, so they are trained first and longest (base). Race-specific
   qualities (running economy at goal pace, lactate handling, neuromuscular
   sharpness) are trained closest to the race because they (a) build faster and
   (b) carry a higher fatigue cost that you don't want lingering on race day.

The taper itself improves performance through measurable physiological changes:
increased blood volume and red-cell mass, restored muscle glycogen, raised muscle
power and contractile function, favourable hormonal shifts, and reduced
psychological fatigue [Mujika & Padilla 2003]. Crucially these are *recovery and
restoration* effects — the taper does not build new fitness, it *reveals* fitness
that fatigue was masking.

## The evidence

- **[Established]** A taper improves endurance performance by ~**0.5–6% (mean
  ~3%)**. Mujika & Padilla's review put the usual improvement near 3%
  [Mujika & Padilla 2003]; Bosquet's meta-analysis of 27 studies reported an
  overall effect size of 0.59 (P<0.001) [Bosquet 2007]. For context, ~2–3% is the
  difference between a 3:00 and a ~2:55 marathon — large relative to almost any
  single workout.

- **[Established]** **The optimal taper cuts volume, not intensity.** Holding
  intensity constant produced a positive effect (ES 0.33), as did holding
  frequency (ES 0.35), whereas the big lever was volume reduction [Bosquet 2007].
  The 2023 endurance meta-analysis agreed: maintaining intensity improved
  performance (SMD −0.55) while *decreasing* intensity showed no benefit, and
  maintaining frequency beat decreasing it [Wang 2023].

- **[Established]** **Best taper dose ≈ 41–60% volume reduction over ~2 weeks**,
  applied as a **progressive (exponential), not step, reduction.** Bosquet: 2-week
  exponential 41–60% cut was most efficient (ES 0.72) [Bosquet 2007]. Wang 2023:
  41–60% reduction was most effective (SMD −0.77), beating <20%, 21–40%, and >60%
  cuts; an 8–14 day taper gave the largest effect (SMD −1.47) though 4–21 days all
  worked. Mujika & Padilla likewise favour progressive non-linear over step
  tapers [Mujika & Padilla 2003].

- **[Probable]** **A short pre-taper overload can amplify the taper.** Wang 2023
  found tapering preceded by a deliberate overload block produced a larger
  time-trial gain than a conventional taper — the "overreach then recover"
  pattern. This is promising but raises injury/illness risk if mistimed.

- **[Established]** **Frequency should stay high during the taper.** Training as
  often as ~80%+ of normal (≤20% frequency reduction) preserves neuromuscular
  feel and economy; large frequency cuts blunt the benefit [Mujika & Padilla
  2003; Wang 2023].

- **[Contested]** **No periodization model is clearly superior for endurance.**
  A meta-analysis found block periodization gave *small* favourable effects over
  traditional for VO₂max (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.02–0.79) and Wmax (SMD 0.28, 95% CI
  0.01–0.54) — but explicitly warned the underlying studies were small and of low
  quality (mean PEDro 3.7/10) [Mølmen 2019]. A well-controlled 12-week trial in
  trained cyclists found **no difference** between block and traditional
  periodization [Almquist 2022]. Treat "block beats linear" as plausible-but-soft,
  not fact.

- **[Contested]** **Periodization theory is more tradition than evidence.** Kiely
  argues the dominant models share an unexamined mid-20th-century heritage and
  rest on assumptions (e.g. rigid, predetermined long-range plans) that are no
  longer scientifically justified; he emphasises individualised, feedback-driven
  load management over template adherence [Kiely 2012].

- **[Probable]** **Undulating ≈ linear when training is equated.** The two
  largest resistance-training meta-analyses both found *no significant difference*
  between linear and daily-undulating periodization: for muscular strength across
  17 studies / 510 participants [Harries, Lubans & Callister 2015], and for muscle
  hypertrophy across 13 studies [Grgic 2017]. Some volume-equated analyses hint at
  a small undulating edge for strength, but it is not robust across reviews.
  Endurance-specific evidence is thin; treat day-to-day undulation (hard/easy
  alternation) as sound load management rather than a magic structure.

- **[Probable]** **Specificity is best timed to peak near the race.** Race-pace
  and event-specific sessions are most valuable in the final build/peak weeks;
  loading them too early wastes their freshness benefit and too little of them
  leaves the runner aerobically fit but unprepared for goal pace. *Evidence type:*
  this is not directly trial-tested as a periodization prescription — it is a
  corollary of the well-established SAID/specificity principle plus near-universal
  practitioner consensus, consistent with the fitness–fatigue rationale for
  late-phase sharpening. Treat the *principle* as solid but the precise *timing*
  window as informed convention rather than a measured optimum.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive — multiple converging meta-analyses):**

- **Taper before goal races.** ~0.5–6% gain, central estimate ~3% (Bosquet 27-study
  meta-analysis, overall ES 0.59, P<0.001; replicated by Wang 2023 endurance MA).
- **Cut volume, hold intensity.** Maintaining intensity helps (Bosquet ES 0.33;
  Wang SMD −0.55); *decreasing* intensity shows no benefit.
- **Dose it ~41–60%, progressively, over ~8–14 days** (Bosquet ES 0.72; Wang SMD
  −0.77 for 41–60%, −1.47 for 8–14 days).
- **Keep frequency high** (≤~20% reduction) — Bosquet ES 0.35; Wang.
- **Do not chase new fitness in the final ~2 weeks** (fitness–fatigue corollary,
  consistent with all taper data).

**Hold loosely (unsettled — small/low-quality, mixed, or principle-derived):**

- **Which periodization model is "best."** Block-vs-traditional effects are small
  and from low-quality studies (Mølmen, mean PEDro 3.7/10); a controlled cyclist
  trial found *no* difference (Almquist 2022). Linear vs undulating is a wash in
  resistance-training MAs (Harries 2015; Grgic 2017) with thin endurance data.
- **Pre-taper overload block.** Promising in one MA subgroup (Wang 2023) but raises
  overreaching/injury risk; experienced, monitored athletes only.
- **Exact specificity-timing prescription.** Principle-derived (SAID) + consensus,
  not directly trial-tested as a timing rule.
- **Recovery-week cadence and individual taper duration.** "Every ~3–5 weeks" [progressive-overload D5] is
  reasonable consensus; individual taper optimum ranges 4 to >28 days — the
  population value is a prior, not a promise.

## How we compute it

Periodization is a *planning* layer, not a single number, but it leans on the
load-modelling metrics Daud already tracks:

- **CTL / ATL / TSB** (chronic load, acute load, training-stress balance) are the
  direct computational analogue of the fitness–fatigue model: CTL ≈ fitness, ATL
  ≈ fatigue, **TSB = CTL − ATL ≈ preparedness/form.** A successful taper is one
  where TSB swings positive (typically into roughly +5 to +25 AU) by race day
  while CTL stays high.
- **ACWR / weekly volume trends** flag whether a phase is progressing (controlled
  overload) or tipping into overreaching.

Implementation notes:

- The base→build→peak→taper *schedule* is **not yet auto-computed** — it is set
  from the runner's goal race date, current fitness, and available weeks.
- **Taper trigger:** count back from race day. Begin volume reduction ~10–14 days
  out for a half/marathon (shorter, ~7–10 days, for 5K–10K where accumulated
  fatigue is lower).
- **Taper shape:** reduce weekly volume progressively **at the dose D1 defines**,
  holding session *intensity* and keeping run *frequency* within ~80–100% of normal.
  *(This read "to ~50–60% of peak (i.e. a ~40–50% cut, within the 41–60% evidence
  window)" — a 40–50% cut is not inside a 41–60% window, and this note stated the taper
  dose three ways. One number, in D1. #100)*
- All of the above are starting estimates to be refined against the individual's
  measured form response (TSB trajectory and subjective freshness), not fixed
  prescriptions.

## How the coach uses it

**Phase logic by stage:**

- **Stage 1 (beginner).** Do not over-engineer. The dominant adaptation is simple
  aerobic base + durability; formal block/undulating schemes are noise at this
  level. Use consistent easy volume, a hard/easy weekly rhythm, and a *light*
  pre-race wind-down (a few easy days). Beginners gain far more from consistency
  than from periodization cleverness.

- **Stage 2 (developing).** Introduce explicit phases: a base mesocycle, a build
  mesocycle adding threshold then VO₂max, and a genuine 7–14 day taper before a
  goal event. Insert a recovery/down week roughly every ~3–5 weeks [progressive-overload D5]. Use TSB to
  confirm the taper is doing its job.

- **Stage 3 (racing/competitive).** Full macrocycle with timed specificity:
  general → specific work, race-pace concentrated in the final 3–6 weeks, an
  evidence-dosed taper (41–60% volume cut, intensity held, ~2 weeks), and
  consideration of a brief functional-overreach block *before* the taper for
  experienced athletes only.

**Decision rules the coach applies:**

- When a goal race is within ~2 weeks → switch to taper logic: cut volume
  progressively, **keep the hard sessions short but at intensity**, do not add new
  fitness, prioritise sleep and fuelling.
- When TSB is deeply negative late in a build → it's *expected* during overload,
  but it must be allowed to rebound during the taper; if it isn't rebounding,
  extend recovery rather than the race-prep work.
- Never "cram": fitness sought inside the final 10–14 days arrives mostly as
  fatigue and degrades, not improves, race-day performance.
- Keep specificity late and general early; resist front-loading race-pace work.

## Honesty & uncertainty

- **The model wars are overstated.** Block vs linear vs undulating differences are
  small, the supporting endurance studies are few and low-quality (PEDro ~3.7/10),
  and at least one controlled cyclist trial found *no* difference [Mølmen 2019;
  Almquist 2022; Kiely 2012]. Daud should not claim a model is "best." Consistency,
  progressive overload, adequate recovery, and a good taper matter far more than
  which periodization label is on the plan.
- **Periodization is tradition-heavy.** Much of the canonical structure descends
  from mid-century Soviet planning, not from RCTs [Kiely 2012]. The strong,
  replicated science is concentrated in the *taper*; the rest is reasonable
  scaffolding.
- **Individual variation in taper response is large.** The "best" taper duration
  ranges from ~4 to >28 days across athletes [Mujika & Padilla 2003]. Some runners
  feel flat on a textbook 2-week taper and race better off a shorter, sharper one;
  others need longer. The population optimum is a *prior*, to be overwritten by the
  individual's history.
- **Taper effect sizes come largely from trained/competitive athletes.** Absolute
  gains in true beginners are less studied and the freshness benefit is smaller
  when chronic fatigue is low to begin with.
- **Day-to-day noise.** Form (TSB) is an estimate, not a measurement; sleep,
  illness, heat, life stress, and fuelling can swamp a +15 TSB on race morning.
  The taper improves the *odds* of a good day, it does not guarantee one.
- **Overload-then-taper is double-edged.** The pre-taper overload that boosts the
  group mean [Wang 2023] also concentrates injury/illness/non-functional-
  overreaching risk; it is for experienced, well-monitored athletes only.
- **Confounders in the literature.** Taper studies vary in how they define volume,
  intensity, and baseline fatigue, and publication bias likely inflates the
  pooled effect somewhat. The ~3% figure is a robust central estimate, not a
  precise promise.

## Safety bounds

- **Do not reduce taper volume by >~60%** of peak for endurance runners — beyond
  this, the added benefit falls away (Bosquet's >60% subgroup and Wang's ≥60%
  subgroup both showed weaker, non-robust effects than the 41–60% band) [Bosquet
  2007; Wang 2023]. The safe, effective window is the **D1 dose** (41–60%).
  (Note: Mujika & Padilla 2003 actually report tolerance for volume cuts up to
  60–90% in some athletes, so the 60% figure is a conservative central guide, not
  a hard physiological cliff.)
- **Never drop intensity to near-zero during the taper.** Maintaining some
  race-pace/quality preserves economy and neuromuscular readiness [Bosquet 2007;
  Wang 2023]. Cutting all intensity is a known way to arrive flat.
- **Cap weekly load progression** during build phases per the training-load doc
  (no abrupt volume spikes / ACWR runaway) — periodization must not become an
  excuse for unsafe ramp rates.
- A pre-taper **overload block is opt-in and gated to experienced athletes** with
  injury/illness monitoring; it is not a default and must never be applied to
  Stage 1 runners.

## Coach Directives

- **D1:** Before a goal race, prescribe a taper that **reduces weekly volume by
  41–60%, progressively, while holding session intensity and keeping run frequency at
  ~80–100% of normal.** — confidence: Established [Bosquet 2007; Wang 2023]
  *(**This is the corpus's ONE canonical taper dose**, and it is stated as a REDUCTION,
  not as a fraction of peak retained. Corrected #100: the corpus said "cut volume by
  41–60%" in five places — all of them the cited ones — and "to ~40–60% of peak" or "to
  ~50–60% of peak" in four others. "To 50–60% of peak" is a 40–50% cut, which falls
  partly outside the evidence window, so the two forms were not interchangeable
  paraphrases of one number. Everywhere else in the corpus now defers here rather than
  restating the figure.)*
- **D2:** Set taper length to **~10–14 days** for half/marathon and **~7–10 days**
  for 5K–10K; allow individual tuning within ~4–21 days based on the runner's
  history. — confidence: Established
- **D3:** **Never seek new fitness inside the final ~10–14 days.** Late hard
  volume adds fatigue, not performance; protect freshness instead. — confidence:
  Established
- **D4:** **Expect and target a positive TSB (form) swing by race day** while
  keeping CTL high; if form is not rebounding during the taper, extend recovery
  rather than add race-prep load. — confidence: Probable
- **D5:** **Concentrate race-specific / goal-pace work in the final ~3–6 weeks**
  (build/peak); keep early phases general and aerobic (specificity timing / SAID).
  — confidence: Probable (SAID principle is solid; the exact timing window is
  consensus-derived, not directly trial-tested)
- **D6:** **Do not assert any periodization model (block/linear/undulating) as
  superior.** Default to a simple linear base→build→peak→taper with a hard/easy
  weekly rhythm; treat block/undulating as optional refinements, not upgrades.
  — confidence: Contested
- **D7:** **Insert a recovery/down week at the cadence `progressive-overload` D5
  defines (~3–5 weeks)** of progressive loading, to let fatigue dissipate
  (supercompensation). D5 owns the number and its provenance; this directive does not
  restate one. — confidence: **Emerging / practitioner consensus** *(was "Probable"
  until 2026-08-01 (#81) — the same claim carried three different confidences and
  three different cadences across the corpus. No study evidences a running deload
  cadence; see D5.)*
- **D8:** **Hard safety cap:** do not cut taper volume by more than ~60% and do
  not zero-out intensity; both reverse the taper benefit. — confidence:
  Established
- **D9:** A **pre-taper overload block is experienced-athlete-only**, monitored,
  and never applied to beginners. — confidence: Probable
- **D10:** For **Stage 1 runners, keep periodization minimal** — consistent easy
  volume, hard/easy rhythm, and a light few-day pre-race wind-down beat any
  formal model. — confidence: Probable
