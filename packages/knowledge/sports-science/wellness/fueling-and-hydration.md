# Fueling & Hydration

## Summary
Carbohydrate is the performance-limiting fuel for runs longer than ~90 minutes,
and exogenous carbohydrate during exercise reliably improves endurance
performance once sessions exceed ~2 hours. The dose-response is well mapped:
~30–60 g/h from a single carbohydrate source, rising to ~90 g/h only when
glucose and fructose are combined (≈2:1) to use two intestinal transporters
[Jeukendrup 2014]. The gut is trainable — high rates must be rehearsed, never
debuted on race day. For fluid, **drinking to thirst** maintains performance and
avoids both dehydration and the genuinely dangerous tail risk of exercise-
associated hyponatremia from over-drinking [Hew-Butler 2015; Hoffman 2016].
Fasted/low-carb training has a narrow, real role for stimulating adaptation but
chronically low carbohydrate availability impairs high-intensity performance and
exercise economy [Burke 2017].

## What it is
"Fueling" is the supply of carbohydrate (and to a lesser extent fat) to working
muscle and the brain during running; "hydration" is the management of body water
and electrolytes (chiefly sodium) across a session. The two are coupled in
practice because most runners take carbohydrate and fluid together (drinks, gels
+ water).

Key quantities the coach reasons about:

- **Carbohydrate during exercise** — grams per hour (g/h) ingested. Typical
  bands: ~30–60 g/h for efforts of 1–2.5 h; up to ~90 g/h for ultra-endurance
  with multiple transportable carbohydrates.
- **Daily carbohydrate** — g per kg body mass per day (g/kg/day), periodised to
  training load: ~3–5 g/kg/day (light), 5–7 (moderate ~1 h/day), 6–10
  (moderate–high 1–3 h/day), 8–12 (very high >4–5 h/day) [Thomas 2016].
- **Endogenous stores** — muscle glycogen (~300–500 g in a trained runner) plus
  liver glycogen (~80–110 g); together roughly 1,600–2,000 kcal, enough for
  ~30 km of running before the "wall" if unfuelled [Rapoport 2010].
- **Fluid** — mL/h, governed primarily by thirst and sweat rate (typically
  0.4–1.8 L/h depending on heat, intensity, body size).
- **Sodium** — the main electrolyte lost in sweat (~20–80 mmol/L, highly
  individual). Serum sodium of 135–145 mmol/L is normal; <135 is hyponatremia.

## Physiology / mechanism
**Why carbohydrate is rate-limiting.** Fat stores are effectively unlimited even
in lean runners, but fat oxidation cannot supply ATP fast enough to sustain
moderate-to-high intensities. Carbohydrate (muscle/liver glycogen + blood
glucose) is the high-flux fuel, and its store is small. As running intensity
rises, the proportion of energy from carbohydrate rises; at marathon pace
(~75–85% VO₂max for trained runners) carbohydrate dominates. When muscle
glycogen falls toward exhaustion the runner cannot maintain pace — the
sudden-onset fatigue colloquially called **"the wall" or "bonking."** Liver
glycogen depletion separately causes hypoglycaemia, adding central/CNS fatigue
[Rapoport 2010].

**Why exogenous carbs help.** Ingested carbohydrate spares liver glycogen,
maintains blood glucose, and sustains a high carbohydrate oxidation rate late in
exercise when endogenous stores run low [Cermak 2013].

**The 60 g/h ceiling and the fructose trick.** Glucose is absorbed across the gut
wall by the SGLT1 transporter, which saturates at an oxidation rate of about
1 g/min (~60 g/h). Ingesting more glucose alone does not raise oxidation — the
surplus sits in the gut and causes distress. Fructose uses a *different*
transporter (GLUT5). Co-ingesting glucose + fructose (~2:1) recruits both
pathways, lifting exogenous carbohydrate oxidation to ~1.2–1.3 g/min (~75 g/h)
and beyond, which is the entire basis for the ~90 g/h "multiple transportable
carbohydrate" recommendation [Jeukendrup 2014].

**Gut trainability.** SGLT1 and GLUT5 transporter density, gastric emptying, and
subjective tolerance all adapt to habitual carbohydrate exposure. Animal work
clearly shows transporter up/down-regulation with diet; in humans the direct
transporter data are scarce, but the downstream outcomes are measurable — a 2023
systematic review of 8 gut-training studies found repeated in-exercise
carbohydrate feeding cut carbohydrate *malabsorption* by ~45–54% and gut
discomfort by ~47% in the studies that responded, alongside higher exogenous
oxidation [Cox 2010; Martinez et al. 2023]. The response is real but variable —
some controlled trials in already-elite athletes find only minor effects [King
et al. 2022].

**Hydration and sodium.** Sweating defends core temperature but loses water and
sodium. Modest dehydration (up to ~2–4% body mass) is tolerated in real-world
running with little to no performance cost; thirst tracks plasma osmolality and
defends it well. The dangerous failure mode is the opposite — drinking *more*
than sweat losses dilutes serum sodium, causing exercise-associated hyponatremia
(EAH), which in severe cases causes cerebral/pulmonary oedema and death
[Hew-Butler 2015].

## The evidence

- **[Established]** Carbohydrate ingestion during prolonged (>2 h)
  moderate-to-high-intensity exercise significantly improves endurance
  performance — a robust, repeatedly replicated finding across decades
  [Cermak & van Loon 2013, *Sports Medicine* review].
- **[Established]** A single carbohydrate source oxidises at up to ~60 g/h
  (≈1 g/min), limited by SGLT1 saturation; this is the recommendation for efforts
  of ~2–3 h [Jeukendrup 2014].
- **[Established]** Glucose+fructose (≈2:1) raises exogenous carbohydrate
  oxidation by up to ~50–75% versus glucose alone, enabling ~90 g/h for
  ultra-endurance; landmark trials show ~8% greater power output over glucose
  alone [Jeukendrup 2014].
- **[Emerging]** Very high intakes (~120 g/h glucose+fructose) may further
  reduce exercise-induced muscle damage and aid recovery versus 60–90 g/h in
  long mountain/ultra events — a small RCT in elite ultra-trail runners (n=20)
  found lower 24 h post-race CK/LDH/GOT at 120 g/h [Viribay et al. 2020]. Graded
  "Emerging": single small study, surrogate (muscle-damage) markers rather than
  performance, and 120 g/h demands substantial gut training and is poorly
  tolerated by many.
- **[Established]** Muscle and liver glycogen are finite (~1,600–2,000 kcal); at
  80–95% VO₂max, modelling and field data converge on glycogen-limited failure
  ("the wall") around mile 21 / ~30 km in the marathon, affecting >40% of
  recreational marathoners [Rapoport 2010].
- **[Established/Probable]** Daily carbohydrate should be periodised to load
  (3–12 g/kg/day); adequate glycogen pre-loading and in-race fueling are the
  primary defences against the wall [Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016, joint
  AND/DC/ACSM position].
- **[Probable]** The gut is trainable: repeated exposure to high carbohydrate
  intake (e.g. ~6.5 g/kg/day for ~28 days, or rehearsed high-g/h sessions)
  increases exogenous carbohydrate oxidation and reduces GI distress. A 2023
  systematic review of 8 studies (mostly male cyclists/runners/triathletes)
  found 2 weeks of in-exercise carbohydrate feeding reduced carbohydrate
  *malabsorption* by ~45–54% and gut discomfort by ~47% in responders [Martinez
  et al. 2023; Cox 2010; Jeukendrup 2017]. Graded "Probable" rather than
  "Established" because the underlying human transporter data are indirect (the
  relevant gut transporters are hard to biopsy), study samples are small, and
  effects are inconsistent — a controlled trial in elite race walkers found only
  minor changes in GI status and performance from a 2-week high-carbohydrate +
  gut-training block, with large individual variability [King et al. 2022].
- **[Established]** Drinking *to thirst* (ad libitum) maintains endurance
  performance versus programmed drinking and avoids both dehydration and
  overhydration; thirst is a sufficient real-time guide for most runners
  [Hoffman, Cotter, Goulet & Laursen 2016]. Ultra-endurance ad-libitum studies
  show no hyponatremia and no performance penalty despite >3% body-mass loss.
- **[Established]** The dominant risk factor for symptomatic/fatal EAH is fluid
  intake in excess of losses (over-drinking); the third International EAH
  Consensus advises against drinking beyond thirst and notes sodium
  supplementation does not reliably prevent EAH if fluid intake is excessive
  [Hew-Butler et al. 2015].
- **[Established]** Chronic ketogenic low-carbohydrate-high-fat (LCHF) diets
  impair exercise economy (raise O₂ cost at race pace) and negate the
  performance gains of intensified training in elite endurance athletes, despite
  raising fat oxidation and VO₂peak — shown in elite race walkers and reproduced
  in a follow-up study by the same group [Burke et al. 2017, *J Physiol*; Burke
  et al. 2020, *PLoS One* replication]. (Note: direct evidence is two studies from
  one research group, ~21 athletes each; the grade rests partly on the
  well-established physiological mechanism — the higher O₂ cost of fat oxidation
  at high intensity — not on independent replication.)
- **[Emerging/Probable]** "Train-low" (performing *selected* sessions with low
  carbohydrate availability) augments molecular training signals — cell
  signalling, gene expression, and oxidative-enzyme/protein content improved in
  ~73–78% of studies — but this translates to measurable performance gains
  inconsistently (~37% of studies showed improvement, ~63% no change) [Bartlett,
  Hawley & Morton 2015]. The "fuel for the work required" framework refines this:
  match carbohydrate to the demands of each session rather than chronically
  restricting [Impey et al. 2018].
- **[Myth]** "You must drink ahead of thirst / as much as possible to avoid
  dehydration." Refuted — over-drinking is the proximate cause of EAH and confers
  no performance benefit; drinking to thirst is the evidence-based default
  [Hoffman 2016; Hew-Butler 2015].
- **[Contested]** Whether routine fasted (overnight) easy runs meaningfully
  improve fat oxidation and body composition in already-trained runners, beyond
  the train-low signalling effect, remains debated and individually variable.

## How we compute it
Daud does not measure glycogen or serum sodium directly. The coach estimates and
prescribes:

- **Target carbs/h** from session duration and intensity (see decision logic
  below). A fuel-plan function (upstream name `fuelPlan()`; the upstream `@daud/core` (a module that exists in no repo — kept as import provenance, not a live dependency)) would map
  (durationHours, intensity, gutTrainedCeiling) → recommended g/h band and
  glucose:fructose guidance. Not yet a ground-truth measurement — it is a
  literature-anchored prescription.
- **Endogenous range** is a coarse estimate: muscle glycogen ≈ 300–500 g in a
  trained runner is assumed, *not* measured; treat as an order-of-magnitude
  guide, refined by how a given runner historically fades in long efforts.
- **Fluid** is delegated to thirst plus an optional sweat-rate estimate
  (pre/post-run body-mass change, if the runner logs it). Sweat rate (L/h) =
  (pre-mass − post-mass + fluid ingested − urine) / hours. This is the only
  individualising fluid measurement and is noisy.
- **Sodium** is not computed; the coach flags risk categories (long duration,
  heat, heavy/salty sweater self-report) rather than prescribing a number.

Flag: every numeric default here is a population starting point. Individual
glycogen capacity, gut tolerance, sweat rate, and sweat-sodium concentration
vary several-fold and must be refined from the runner's own logged data.

## How the coach uses it
Decision logic, by **session duration** (the primary driver) and **stage**:

**Carbohydrate during the run**
- **< 45–60 min:** no fueling needed for fuel's sake. For hard/quality efforts a
  carbohydrate mouth-rinse or small amount can give a small CNS benefit
  [Jeukendrup 2014]. Default: none.
- **~60–150 min:** ~30–60 g/h, single source acceptable. Start early
  (within the first ~30–45 min), do not wait for hunger or fade.
- **> 2.5–3 h / ultra:** up to ~90 g/h, **only** as glucose+fructose (~2:1) to
  beat the SGLT1 ceiling, and **only** if the gut has been trained to it.
- Always pair gels/concentrated carbohydrate with water unless using an
  appropriately dilute drink.

**Glycogen / the wall**
- For goal runs ≥ ~90 min, ensure high pre-run glycogen (carbohydrate-rich days
  beforehand; a full glycogen-loading protocol only for races ≥ ~90 min–2 h).
- Treat the marathon wall as preventable: pre-loading + in-race fueling +
  appropriate (not over-fast) early pacing [Rapoport 2010; Thomas 2016].

**Gut training**
- Never debut a new race-day fueling rate or product on race day. Rehearse target
  g/h in long runs for several weeks; progress intake gradually [Jeukendrup 2017].
- If a runner reports GI distress at target g/h, lower the rate, shift toward
  glucose+fructose, reduce concentration, and build tolerance over weeks.

**Hydration**
- Default instruction: **drink to thirst.** Do not prescribe forced schedules for
  general training [Hoffman 2016].
- In heat or very long efforts, encourage an estimate of sweat rate but keep
  thirst as the governing signal; weight perceived effort and pace alongside any
  fluid plan.
- Explicitly warn against over-drinking; gaining weight during a run is a red
  flag for EAH risk [Hew-Butler 2015].

**Sodium**
- For runs < ~2 h in temperate conditions, plain water + normal diet suffices.
- For long/hot efforts, or self-identified salty/heavy sweaters, suggest sodium-
  containing drinks/foods — framed as comfort, cramp-tolerance, and palatability,
  not as an EAH prophylactic (it isn't a reliable one) [Hew-Butler 2015].

**Fasted / low-carb**
- Permit *occasional* easy fasted or low-glycogen sessions as a deliberate
  adaptation stimulus for experienced runners (Stage 2–3), under the "fuel for
  the work required" principle [Impey 2018].
- Do **not** prescribe chronic ketogenic/LCHF dieting for performance-focused
  runners — it impairs economy and high-intensity output [Burke 2017].
- Always fuel quality/long key sessions and races with high carbohydrate
  availability.

**Stage differences**
- **Stage 1 (beginner):** keep it simple — water to thirst; carbohydrate only on
  runs beyond ~75–90 min; no gut-training or train-low complexity.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** introduce in-run carbohydrate on long runs, begin gut
  training toward race rates, optional occasional fasted easy runs.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** periodised daily carbohydrate, rehearsed race fueling at
  goal g/h with glucose+fructose, sweat-rate-informed but thirst-governed
  hydration, deliberate carbohydrate periodisation around key sessions.

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **Large individual variation.** Gut tolerance, sweat rate, and sweat-sodium
  concentration vary several-fold between runners; published g/h and mL/h numbers
  are population means, not personal prescriptions. Some runners comfortably
  oxidise >90 g/h after training; others get GI distress at 60.
- **Glycogen is estimated, not measured.** "~30 km / mile 21" for the wall is a
  modelled and population average [Rapoport 2010]; trained, fat-adapted, or
  well-fuelled runners shift it substantially. Daud never knows a runner's actual
  glycogen state.
- **Gut-training mechanism is partly inferred.** Direct human transporter data are
  scarce because measuring SGLT1/GLUT5 density requires gut biopsy; the
  tolerance/malabsorption evidence is reasonable (systematic-review level) but
  the studies are small and inconsistent, and the cellular detail is
  extrapolated from animal work [Martinez et al. 2023; Jeukendrup 2017]. In
  already-elite athletes a structured 2-week protocol produced only minor
  changes with wide individual variation [King et al. 2022]. Hence "Probable,"
  not "Established."
- **Train-low is genuinely unsettled for performance.** Molecular adaptations are
  consistent, but only a minority of studies show actual performance gains, and
  the downside (compromised session quality, immune/overreaching risk) is real
  [Bartlett 2015]. The coach should hedge accordingly.
- **Dehydration vs performance is nuanced.** The claim that up to ~2–4% body-mass
  loss is performance-neutral comes largely from real-world (vs lab cycling-to-
  thermoneutral) studies; in extreme heat the tolerable margin is smaller, and
  thermoregulatory strain still rises. Thirst remains the best individual guide.
- **EAH is rare but catastrophic.** It is low-incidence, so most runners never
  see it — but it has killed marathoners, and the failure mode is the
  *well-intentioned* advice to "stay ahead of thirst." This asymmetry justifies a
  hard guardrail even though the event is uncommon.
- **Day-to-day noise.** Appetite, GI tolerance, and fluid needs shift with sleep,
  heat, menstrual phase, illness, and recent meals. A single bad fueling day is
  not signal; persistent patterns are.
- **What's unknown:** the true upper ceiling of trainable carbohydrate oxidation
  (recent work explores 120 g/h, with early signals on reduced muscle damage but
  not yet performance [Viribay et al. 2020]), the long-term health/performance
  trade-offs of habitual very-high carbohydrate fueling, and the optimal
  personalised train-low dose.

## Safety bounds
These are hard limits the coach must respect. **The over-drinking bound is enforced in
code; the rest are not** — see the per-bullet note at the end of this section.

- **Never advise drinking beyond thirst / to a schedule that exceeds sweat
  losses.** Over-drinking is the proximate cause of exercise-associated
  hyponatremia, which can be fatal. If a runner reports weight *gain* during a
  run, treat as an EAH red flag [Hew-Butler 2015].
- **Never present sodium supplementation as a guaranteed prevention for EAH** —
  it is not, if fluid intake is excessive [Hew-Butler 2015].
- **Symptomatic red flags during/after a long or hot effort** — confusion,
  disproportionate headache, nausea/vomiting, seizure, altered consciousness —
  are medical emergencies (possible EAH or heat illness); the coach must direct
  the runner to stop and seek urgent medical care, not give a fueling tip.
- **Do not prescribe chronic ketogenic/severe carbohydrate restriction** to a
  runner pursuing endurance performance; flag the economy/performance cost and,
  for under-fuelling patterns, the risk of Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport
  (RED-S).
- **Do not introduce novel high-g/h fueling on race day.** Untrained high intake
  risks race-ending GI distress.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Carbohydrate ingestion improves endurance performance in efforts beyond ~2 h —
  decades of replicated RCTs and review consensus [Cermak & van Loon 2013].
- The dose map is settled: ~30–60 g/h from a single source (SGLT1-limited at
  ~1 g/min), rising to ~90 g/h **only** as glucose+fructose (~2:1) using two
  transporters [Jeukendrup 2014].
- Endogenous carbohydrate is finite and the marathon "wall" (~30 km) is real and
  largely preventable with pre-loading, in-race fuelling, and disciplined early
  pacing [Rapoport 2010; Thomas 2016].
- **Drink to thirst.** It maintains performance and avoids both dehydration and
  over-drinking; never coach drinking ahead of thirst [Hoffman 2016].
- Over-drinking — not under-drinking — is the proximate cause of
  exercise-associated hyponatremia, and sodium does not reliably prevent it if
  fluid intake is excessive [Hew-Butler 2015]. (Safety-critical.)
- Chronic ketogenic/LCHF dieting impairs running economy and blunts the gains of
  intensified training — do not prescribe it for performance goals [Burke 2017,
  reproduced Burke 2020].

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- Gut training works and is worth doing, but the cellular mechanism in humans is
  inferred from animal/indirect data — confidence is "Probable," not proven
  [Jeukendrup 2017].
- "Train-low" reliably drives molecular adaptations but only inconsistently
  improves actual performance (~37% of studies), with real downside risk; treat
  as an individualised tool, not a default [Bartlett 2015; Impey 2018].
- The true trainable ceiling of carbohydrate oxidation (recent work probes
  120 g/h), the precise tolerable dehydration margin in extreme heat, and the
  value of routine fasted easy runs in already-trained runners are all open
  questions — individualise from the runner's own response.

## Healthee implementation & honesty policy
- **No `derived_daily` field of its own (`applies_to_metrics: []`).** Daud does not measure
  glycogen or serum sodium; fueling/hydration is a *prescription* layer. The body's
  upstream names (`carbsPerHour` / `fluidPerHour` / `sessionDurationHours` /
  `sessionIntensity`, and a `fuelPlan()`-style mapping) name the literature-anchored
  recommendation the coach generates from session duration/intensity — none is a Healthee
  `derived_daily` field today. Sweat rate = (pre-mass − post-mass + fluid ingested −
  urine)/hours is the only individualising fluid measurement, and it is noisy and opt-in.
- **Safety-critical guardrails that WOULD be worth mirroring in
  code (see *Safety bounds* for what is and is not enforced today — #87):**
  - **Never advise drinking beyond thirst / in excess of sweat losses** — over-drinking is
    the proximate cause of exercise-associated **hyponatremia**, which can be fatal; in-run
    **weight gain is an EAH red flag** (D8, SAFETY-CRITICAL). **Sodium supplementation is not
    a reliable EAH prophylactic** if fluid intake is excessive.
  - On **confusion, severe headache, vomiting, seizure, or altered consciousness** during/
    after long or hot efforts, **stop fueling advice and direct the runner to urgent medical
    care** (possible EAH or heat illness) (D12, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
  - **Do not prescribe chronic ketogenic/severe carbohydrate restriction** for
    endurance-performance goals; flag the economy cost and, for under-fuelling patterns,
    **RED-S** risk (cross-link `menstrual_cycle_and_training`, `injury_prevention`).
- **Honesty rules (carry into UI + LLM):**
  - The **carbohydrate dose map is settled** (30–60 g/h single-source; up to ~90 g/h **only**
    as glucose+fructose ~2:1) and stated plainly; **gut training is "Probable"** (human
    mechanism inferred) and **train-low is unsettled for performance** (only ~37% of studies
    show gains) — hedge accordingly.
  - Every g/h, mL/h and the ~30 km "wall" are **population starting points**; gut tolerance,
    sweat rate and sweat-sodium vary several-fold and must be refined from the runner's own
    logged data. Daud **never knows a runner's actual glycogen or sodium state**.
  - **Never debut a new race-day fueling rate or product on race day.**
