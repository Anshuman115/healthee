# Menstrual Cycle & Training

## Summary
Across the menstrual cycle, oestrogen and progesterone fluctuate and plausibly
nudge metabolism, thermoregulation and tissue laxity — but the headline question
"does cycle phase change running performance?" has a clear, honest answer: **on
average the effect is trivial and the between-individual variation is large, on
low-quality evidence** [McNulty 2020]. The same is true for oral contraceptives
[Elliott-Sale 2020]. So Daud does **not** prescribe a generic phase-based plan.
What *is* well established and safety-critical is the downside of under-fuelling:
low energy availability disrupts the cycle and, sustained, drives Relative Energy
Deficiency in Sport (REDs) with real bone, endocrine and performance harm
[Mountjoy 2023; Loucks 2003]. The coaching stance is therefore individualised,
symptom-led and energy-aware — track the runner's *own* response, treat a missing
or newly irregular period as a red flag, and never force a textbook cycle map
onto her.

## What it is
The **menstrual cycle** is the ~21–35 day (commonly ~28 day) hormonal cycle of a
naturally menstruating ("eumenorrheic") person, driven by the
hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. It is conventionally split into phases by
the dominant ovarian hormones:

- **Early follicular** (≈ days 1–5, menses): oestrogen and progesterone both
  **low**. Day 1 = first day of bleeding.
- **Late follicular** (≈ days 6–14): **oestrogen rises** to a pre-ovulatory peak;
  progesterone still low.
- **Ovulation** (≈ day 14, mid-cycle): triggered by a luteinising hormone (LH)
  surge; oestrogen falls back from its peak.
- **Luteal** (≈ days 15–28): the corpus luteum raises **progesterone** (and a
  secondary oestrogen rise); both fall in the late luteal phase if no pregnancy,
  triggering menses and the next cycle.

Key practical quantities for the coach:

- **Cycle phase / cycle day** — only meaningful if the runner tracks it, and only
  *reliably* phased with ovulation confirmation (LH kits + luteal progesterone),
  which most app-based tracking lacks [Elliott-Sale 2021].
- **Energy availability (EA)** — dietary energy intake minus exercise energy
  expenditure, normalised to fat-free mass: `EA = (kcal in − exercise kcal) / kg
  FFM / day`. ~45 kcal/kg FFM/day is "healthy"; sustained low values are the
  upstream cause of cycle disruption and REDs [Loucks 2003; Mountjoy 2023].
- **Hormonal contraception (HC)** — oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), hormonal
  IUDs, implants, injections, patches, rings. OCPs *suppress* the body's own
  oestrogen/progesterone and supply exogenous synthetic hormones on a fixed
  schedule, flattening the natural cycle.
- **Symptom load** — self-reported menstrual symptoms (cramps, fatigue, mood,
  bloating, headache, breast tenderness). This is frequently the most
  *actionable* signal, more so than phase per se [Bruinvels 2021].

## Physiology / mechanism
Oestrogen (oestradiol) and progesterone have receptors well beyond the
reproductive tract — in muscle, bone, brain, blood vessels and connective tissue —
so plausible mechanisms exist by which the cycle *could* affect training:

- **Substrate metabolism.** Oestrogen tends to promote fat oxidation and spare
  glycogen; progesterone is broadly catabolic/glycogen-favouring. The net effect
  across the luteal phase is small and mostly relevant only in prolonged,
  low-to-moderate intensity exercise [Oosthuyse & Bosch 2010, mechanism review].
- **Thermoregulation.** Progesterone raises resting/basal body temperature by
  ~0.3–0.5 °C in the luteal phase and shifts thermoregulatory set-points, which
  could matter for heat tolerance and fluid balance during hard sessions in the
  heat — a plausible but small effect.
- **Connective tissue laxity.** Oestrogen affects collagen and ligament laxity;
  this underpins the hypothesis that ACL/soft-tissue injury risk varies by phase
  (see Evidence) [Herzberg 2017].
- **Fluid and cardiovascular.** Sex hormones modulate plasma volume, sodium
  handling and resting heart rate / HRV across the cycle — a known confounder
  when interpreting daily readiness metrics, not necessarily a performance driver.
- **Energy availability → reproductive axis.** The best-understood mechanism here
  is *downstream*, not phase-based: when energy availability falls below ~30
  kcal/kg FFM/day, LH pulsatility is disrupted within days, suppressing the
  ovarian axis and ultimately menstruation [Loucks 2003]. This is the physiological
  spine of REDs.

The crucial caveat: a plausible mechanism is **not** a demonstrated performance
effect. Hormone concentrations swing severalfold, but the systems they act on are
buffered and redundant, so group-level performance changes are typically washed
out — which is exactly what the meta-analytic evidence shows.

## The evidence

**Phase effect on performance — the central question.**

- **[Contested]** Across the menstrual cycle, exercise performance is *trivially*
  reduced in the early follicular phase versus other phases, with **large
  between-study variation** and **low-quality evidence** — *systematic review and
  meta-analysis of 78 studies (network meta-analysis of 73 studies, 954
  participants; pairwise of 51 studies, 709 participants)* [McNulty 2020]. The
  pooled effect was tiny: early follicular vs all other phases ES ≈ −0.06 (95%
  CrI −0.16 to 0.04); the largest contrast, early vs late follicular, ES ≈ −0.14
  (−0.26 to −0.03) — both **trivial**. Study quality: only 8% high, 24% moderate,
  42% low, 26% very low, chiefly because most studies never verified cycle phase
  with hormones. The authors' own conclusion: adopt an **individualised approach**,
  not generic guidelines. Graded **Contested** because mechanisms and many
  individuals' lived experience suggest *something*, while the best aggregate
  evidence shows the average effect is negligible and the data are weak.

- **[Probable]** Most athletes *perceive* their cycle affects training and
  performance, even though measured group effects are trivial. In a survey of
  **1,086 athletes across 57 sports**, 76% believed their cycle or HC could affect
  training performance and 73% competitive performance [Ekenros 2022,
  *cross-sectional survey*]. In **6,812 exercising women** (Strava), participants
  reported on average ~11 cycle-related symptoms, and symptom prevalence/frequency
  was associated with reduced availability to train and compete [Bruinvels 2021,
  *large observational survey*]. The perception and symptom burden are real and
  consistent even where the ergometric effect is not — which is *why* a
  symptom-led, individualised approach beats a phase-template approach.

**Hormonal contraception.**

- **[Contested]** Oral contraceptives produce, at most, a **trivial** group-level
  reduction in performance versus naturally menstruating women — *systematic
  review and meta-analysis of 42 studies, 590 participants* [Elliott-Sale 2020].
  Between-group effects were trivial (OCP consumption vs other MC phases ES ≈
  0.13, 95% CrI −0.05 to 0.28; OCP withdrawal vs early follicular ES ≈ 0.18, −0.02
  to 0.37). 83% of studies were moderate, low or very low quality. Conclusion:
  any group effect is trivial and an **individualised approach** is warranted. The
  coach should treat HC as performance-neutral on average while watching the
  individual.

- **[Established]** OCPs reliably *flatten* the endogenous hormonal cycle
  (suppressing the runner's own oestradiol/progesterone and supplying synthetic
  hormones on a fixed schedule) — basic, well-replicated endocrinology
  [Elliott-Sale 2020, mechanism]. Practically: a runner on combined OCPs does not
  have meaningful "natural" cycle phases to train around; her hormonal pattern
  follows the pill packet. **Important:** OCPs can mask the loss of natural
  menstruation, hiding a REDs warning sign — withdrawal bleeds on the pill are not
  evidence of energy adequacy or bone health [Mountjoy 2023].

**Energy availability, the cycle, and REDs — the part that is solid.**

- **[Established]** Low energy availability disrupts reproductive function. The
  seminal *controlled laboratory experiment* dropped EA to 10, 20, 30 vs 45
  kcal/kg FFM/day for 5 days and showed LH pulsatility was disrupted below ~30
  kcal/kg FFM/day [Loucks & Thuma 2003, n=29 regularly menstruating women]. This
  is the mechanistic basis for exercise-associated menstrual dysfunction.

- **[Established]** Sustained low energy availability — whether intentional or
  inadvertent — causes **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)**, impairing
  menstrual function, bone mineral density, metabolic and endocrine health, immune
  function, and ultimately performance — *IOC consensus statement* synthesising a
  large body of evidence [Mountjoy 2023]. LEA sits on a spectrum from adaptable to
  problematic; amenorrhea and low BMD are red-flag indicators. This supersedes the
  older "Female Athlete Triad" framing and applies to all genders.

- **[Contested]** The "**30 kcal/kg FFM/day threshold**" is a useful heuristic but
  **not a hard switch**. Free-living and retrospective work shows menstrual
  disturbances occurring both above and below it, and that the *magnitude/frequency
  of energy deficit* predicts disturbance frequency better than a clean threshold
  [Williams 2015, *controlled feeding/exercise study*; threshold critiques in the
  EA literature]. The coach should treat 30 kcal/kg FFM/day as a caution zone, not
  a precise cliff, and weight cumulative deficit and symptoms.

**Injury risk across the cycle.**

- **[Contested]** ACL laxity appears higher around ovulation, and a *systematic
  review and meta-analysis of 6 laxity studies plus injury data* found more ACL
  injuries than expected in the ovulatory/pre-ovulatory phase and the fewest in
  the luteal phase — but on **very low strength of evidence** with bias and
  confounding concerns [Herzberg 2017]. More recent reviews call the link
  inconclusive. OCP use may slightly lower ACL injury risk (~20% in some analyses),
  again low-certainty. Relevant mostly to multidirectional/team sports; for steady
  running the direct applicability is limited. Hold loosely.

**Methodology — why so much of this is "low quality."**

- **[Established]** The dominant reason cycle/HC performance research is weak is
  **poor phase verification**: most studies define phase by calendar counting or
  self-report without confirming ovulation (LH surge) or measuring progesterone,
  so "phases" are frequently mislabelled [Elliott-Sale 2021, methodological
  standards paper]. Until high-quality, hormone-verified studies accumulate, firm
  phase-based prescriptions are not justified — a key reason Daud stays
  individualised.

## How we compute it
Daud does **not** infer cycle phase from physiology and does **not** auto-generate
a phase-based plan. Inputs are opt-in and runner-supplied:

- **Cycle tracking (optional):** if the runner logs cycle start dates, the coach
  may surface *her own* historical patterns (e.g. "you've reported heavier-legged
  easy runs in the days before your period") — pattern reflection, **not** a
  population template. Calendar-only phasing is explicitly flagged as approximate
  and unverified [Elliott-Sale 2021].
- **Symptom load (optional):** logged symptoms (cramps, fatigue, sleep, mood) feed
  the normal readiness/adjustment logic the same way poor sleep or high stress do —
  symptom-led, not phase-led.
- **Energy availability:** Daud does not measure EA precisely (it needs accurate
  intake + exercise expenditure + FFM). The coach instead screens for **REDs risk
  signals**: unexplained loss of menstruation or new irregularity (off HC),
  involuntary weight/BMD loss, stress fractures, persistent fatigue/underperformance,
  rapid intentional energy restriction alongside rising load. ~30 kcal/kg FFM/day
  is a caution heuristic if EA is estimable, never a precise gate [Loucks 2003;
  Williams 2015; Mountjoy 2023].
- **Daily metrics confound:** resting HR and HRV shift across the cycle (often
  higher RHR / altered HRV in the luteal phase). Readiness logic must treat cycle phase
  as a **known confounder** of these metrics, not over-react to a single luteal-phase
  reading. (This said "`@daud/core` readiness logic must …" — a module that exists
  nowhere in this repo, the legacy repo or git history. Healthee's readiness logic is
  `derive/recovery.py` + `read/recovery_guidance.py`, and **neither knows about cycle
  phase**: nothing is collected, so nothing adjusts for it. This is a rule for the coach,
  not a code behaviour. Corrected 2026-08-01, #87.)

No cycle metric here is ground truth; all are self-reported and individually
calibrated.

## How the coach uses it
Decision logic is **individualised and symptom-led**, by stage:

**Default stance (all stages).**
- Do **not** impose a generic "train hard in the follicular phase, back off in the
  luteal phase" plan — the average phase effect is trivial and individual variation
  dominates [McNulty 2020].
- If a runner *wants* to track and finds reliable personal patterns, support
  adjusting *her* hard sessions around *her* worst symptom days — framed as
  individual response, with explicit uncertainty.
- Treat menstrual **symptoms** like any other readiness input: a runner with
  disabling cramps/fatigue gets the same flexibility as one who slept badly — shift
  or soften the session, don't cancel the plan.

**Hormonal contraception.**
- For runners on combined OCPs, do not build a "natural cycle" plan — their
  hormones follow the packet. Treat performance as effectively phase-neutral and
  coach off symptoms and data [Elliott-Sale 2020].
- Never reassure a runner that a withdrawal bleed on the pill confirms energy
  adequacy or bone health [Mountjoy 2023].

**Energy availability / REDs (safety-critical).**
- Screen proactively in any runner increasing load while restricting intake or
  losing weight.
- A **missing period, or new menstrual irregularity** in a non-HC runner is a red
  flag — surface it, explain the REDs link, and recommend professional evaluation;
  do not "train through" it.
- Never prescribe or endorse weight loss / energy restriction as a performance
  strategy; frame adequate fuelling as performance- and health-protective
  [Mountjoy 2023]. Cross-link `fueling-and-hydration`.

**Stage differences.**
- **Stage 1 (beginner):** keep it simple — normalise that symptoms can affect
  sessions, allow flexibility, no cycle-syncing complexity. Baseline REDs
  awareness.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** optional cycle/symptom tracking to discover *personal*
  patterns; begin treating cycle as a confounder of HRV/RHR readiness.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** individualised symptom management around key sessions and
  race day (including symptom-relief strategies and, with a clinician, HC timing
  for athletes who choose it); heightened REDs vigilance because high load + lean
  pressure raises risk.

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **The headline effect is genuinely small and the evidence genuinely weak.** The
  best meta-analysis finds only a *trivial* average phase effect on low-quality
  data with large heterogeneity [McNulty 2020]. We must not inflate this into "your
  cycle controls your performance" — nor into "the cycle never matters." Both
  overstate the evidence.
- **Group-trivial ≠ individually-irrelevant.** A trivial *average* with large
  variance means some individuals plausibly *do* experience meaningful effects
  while many experience none. This is the core reason for individualisation — the
  population mean is the wrong unit for one runner [McNulty 2020; Ekenros 2022].
- **Perception vs measurement gap.** ~73–76% of athletes believe their cycle
  affects performance [Ekenros 2022], far exceeding measured ergometric effects.
  Perception and symptom burden are real and worth coaching to — but the coach
  should not present perceived effects as proven physiology.
- **Phase identification is the field's central flaw.** Most studies (and almost
  all consumer apps) phase by calendar, not by confirmed ovulation, so "luteal" or
  "follicular" labels are often wrong [Elliott-Sale 2021]. Daud's own calendar
  tracking inherits this imprecision and must say so.
- **The threshold is fuzzy.** 30 kcal/kg FFM/day is a heuristic, not a hard line;
  disturbances occur on both sides and cumulative deficit matters more [Williams
  2015]. Treat it as a caution zone.
- **HC heterogeneity.** "Hormonal contraception" spans many formulations
  (monophasic/triphasic pills, progestin-only, IUD, implant, injection) with
  different physiology; most research is on combined OCPs, so conclusions don't
  cleanly transfer to all methods [Elliott-Sale 2020].
- **Daily-metric confounding.** Cycle-driven shifts in RHR/HRV can be mistaken for
  fatigue or illness. The coach must not over-react to a single luteal-phase
  reading.
- **What we still don't know:** which individuals are true "responders," whether
  any phase-targeted programming actually improves outcomes (almost no
  high-quality intervention trials exist), and the precise EA thresholds per
  individual. These are open questions, not settled science.

## Safety bounds
Hard limits the coach must respect. **One is partly enforced in code; the rest are not**
— see the per-bullet note at the end of this section.

- **Amenorrhea / new menstrual irregularity is a medical red flag.** For a
  non-HC runner, a missing or newly irregular cycle must trigger a REDs/health
  concern: surface it, explain the link to low energy availability and bone health,
  and recommend evaluation by a qualified clinician. Never frame it as a normal or
  desirable sign of training [Mountjoy 2023; Loucks 2003].
- **Never prescribe, endorse, or normalise low energy availability / weight loss
  as a performance tactic.** Sustained under-fuelling causes REDs (bone loss,
  endocrine and metabolic harm, impaired performance) [Mountjoy 2023].
- **Do not use a withdrawal bleed on hormonal contraception as evidence of energy
  adequacy or bone health** — it can mask REDs [Mountjoy 2023].
- **Stay in scope.** Daud is a running coach, not a gynaecologist. For contraceptive
  choice, suspected REDs, persistent menstrual problems, severe dysmenorrhea, or
  any clinical question, advise consulting a healthcare professional rather than
  giving medical direction.
- **No firm phase-based performance prescriptions.** The evidence does not support
  them [McNulty 2020]; the coach must not present cycle-syncing as proven.

**What is actually enforced (#87, corrected 2026-08-01).** This section opened "Mirrored
as guardrails in `@daud/core`; the AI may not override them" — `@daud/core` is a module
that exists nowhere in this repo, in `~/projects/healthee-legacy`, or in git history; the
phrase arrived with the upstream sports-science corpus import. The true position:

- **The low-energy-availability bound is PARTLY enforced.**
  `insights/output_guard.py`'s hand-compiled `advise_through_bone_stress_or_reds` blocks
  an answer whose sentence mentions RED-S, low energy availability, relative energy
  deficiency or bone stress *and* advises losing weight, eating less, cutting intake or
  adding load. That covers the "never normalise under-fuelling as a performance tactic"
  clause when those words are present; a sentence that normalises weight loss without
  naming any of them does not fire. Separately, `insights/refusals.py` refuses a
  *question* mentioning an eating disorder, anorexia or bulimia before the model runs.
- **The amenorrhea red flag, the withdrawal-bleed rule, the stay-in-scope rule and the
  no-phase-prescription rule are NOT enforced in code** — they are rules for the coach to

  for that mechanism, not users of it.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- The *average* effect of menstrual cycle phase on performance is **trivial**, with
  large individual variation, on low-quality evidence — so generic cycle-syncing is
  not justified [McNulty 2020].
- Oral contraceptives are, on average, **performance-neutral** (at most a trivial
  effect) and flatten the natural cycle [Elliott-Sale 2020].
- **Low energy availability disrupts the menstrual cycle and, sustained, causes
  REDs** with real bone/endocrine/performance harm — this is the safety-critical
  core [Loucks 2003; Mountjoy 2023].
- A missing or newly irregular period (off HC) is a **red flag**, not a training
  badge [Mountjoy 2023].
- The right operating mode is **individualised and symptom-led**, because the
  population mean does not describe any single runner [McNulty 2020; Elliott-Sale
  2021].

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- Whether *this* runner has a meaningful phase response — possible for some, absent
  for many; only her own tracked data can tell, and even that is imprecise
  [McNulty 2020; Elliott-Sale 2021].
- The exact 30 kcal/kg FFM/day EA threshold — a caution heuristic, not a hard line
  [Williams 2015].
- Phase-linked ACL/injury risk — biologically plausible but very low-certainty and
  most relevant to multidirectional sport [Herzberg 2017].
- That perceived effects (felt by ~3 in 4 athletes) reflect proven physiology —
  real and worth coaching to, but not established ergometrically [Ekenros 2022].

## Healthee implementation & honesty policy
- **No `derived_daily` field of its own (`applies_to_metrics: []`).** Daud does **not** infer
  cycle phase from physiology and does **not** auto-generate a phase-based plan; all inputs
  are opt-in and runner-supplied. The body's `@daud/core` quantities (`energyAvailability` /
  `cyclePhase` / `perceivedSymptomLoad`) are self-reported/estimated, not Healthee
  `derived_daily` fields; calendar-only phasing is explicitly flagged as approximate and
  unverified.
- **Confound to encode:** **resting HR and HRV shift across the cycle** (often higher RHR /
  altered HRV in the luteal phase). Readiness logic must treat cycle phase as a **known
  confounder** and not over-react to a single luteal-phase reading (D9) — this is the
  product's honesty stance for `recovery_readiness` / `heart_rate_variability` inputs.
- **Safety-critical guardrails that WOULD be worth mirroring in
  code (see *Safety bounds* for what is and is not enforced today — #87):**
  - **Amenorrhea / new menstrual irregularity in a non-HC runner is a red flag** — surface
    it, explain the low-energy-availability & bone-health link, and recommend clinician
    evaluation; never frame it as a normal/desirable training sign (D5, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
  - **Never prescribe, endorse, or normalise low energy availability / weight loss as a
    performance tactic** — sustained under-fuelling causes **REDs** (D6, SAFETY-CRITICAL);
    **do not treat a withdrawal bleed on hormonal contraception as evidence of energy
    adequacy or bone health** (D7). **Stay in scope** — refer contraceptive choice / suspected
    REDs / persistent menstrual problems to a healthcare professional (D10, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
    Cross-links: `fueling_and_hydration`, `injury_prevention`.
- **Honesty rules (carry into UI + LLM):**
  - Calibrate to the evidence: the **average cycle-phase performance effect is trivial on
    low-quality data** — **no generic cycle-syncing plan** (D1); but **group-trivial ≠
    individually-irrelevant**, so support *her own* tracked patterns with explicit
    uncertainty. Never inflate into "your cycle controls your performance," nor deny it can
    matter for some.
  - **30 kcal/kg FFM/day is a caution heuristic, not a hard threshold** (Contested); weight
    cumulative deficit and symptoms.
