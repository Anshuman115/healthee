# Running Injury Prevention

## Summary
Running injuries are common — roughly **7.7 injuries per 1000 hours** in recreational
runners and far higher (~17.8/1000 h) in novices [Videbæk 2015] — and the great
majority are **overuse** injuries driven by training load outrunning tissue
capacity, not by a single traumatic event. The single most actionable lever is
**load management**: avoid large, abrupt spikes in weekly volume/intensity and let
the runner's own recent training set the ceiling. The popular **"10% per week" rule
and the ACWR are useful *framing* but weak as precise prescriptions** — the one RCT
of the 10% rule found no protective effect, and ACWR has serious methodological
criticism. The best-evidenced active interventions are **strength training** (halves
overuse-injury risk in broad meta-analysis) and, for specific knee/shin problems,
**increasing cadence** to cut joint loading. **Bone-stress injuries** sit partly
outside the load model: they are strongly tied to **low energy availability / RED-S**,
so under-fuelling (especially with menstrual dysfunction or low testosterone) is a
red flag the coach must act on. After any bone-stress injury, return to running must
be **graduated, walk-run based, and symptom-guided** — never rushed.

## What it is
"Running injury prevention" is the set of training, biomechanical, and health
practices that keep a runner below the threshold where repeated loading causes
musculoskeletal breakdown. The relevant quantities:

- **Injury incidence** — running-related injuries (RRIs) per **1000 hours** of running
  (the standard exposure-normalised metric), or per 1000 km, or as a season
  prevalence. An RRI is usually defined as a running-related musculoskeletal pain
  causing a restriction of or stop in running for ≥1 day (definitions vary, which
  matters — see Honesty).
- **Most common sites/diagnoses** in runners: knee (the single most affected
  region, ~25–30%), then lower leg/shin, foot, Achilles/calf. Frequent diagnoses:
  patellofemoral pain ("runner's knee"), medial tibial stress syndrome ("shin
  splints"), Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciitis, iliotibial band syndrome,
  and bone-stress injuries/stress fractures [Videbæk 2015; Lopes 2012].
- **Training load** — the dose: weekly volume (km or min), intensity distribution,
  and the *rate of change* of that dose. Captured as weekly-distance progression
  (%) and the acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) — see `training-load-acwr`.
- **Energy availability (EA)** — dietary energy intake minus exercise energy
  expenditure, normalised to fat-free mass (kcal/kg FFM/day). The threshold of
  concern is **<30 kcal/kg FFM/day** (vs ~45 optimal) — central to bone health.

Typical ranges by population: **novices** are the highest-risk group (17–33/1000 h),
**recreational** runners ~7–8/1000 h, **competitive long-distance/track** runners
often lower per-hour (down to ~2.5/1000 h) but with high *bone-stress* burden because
of volume and intensity [Videbæk 2015].

## Physiology / mechanism
**The load–capacity model.** Bone, tendon, muscle, and fascia all remodel in
response to mechanical load: an appropriate stimulus followed by adequate recovery
*increases* tissue capacity (Wolff's law for bone; collagen turnover for tendon).
The same tissues fail when the applied load repeatedly exceeds current capacity
before remodelling can catch up. Overuse injury is therefore fundamentally a
**mismatch between the rate of loading and the rate of adaptation**, not an
intrinsic "weakness." This is why *sudden change* in load — more than absolute load
— is the recurring villain: a runner adapted to 40 km/week is not adapted to 60.

**Why bone is special.** Bone responds to load with an initial *resorption* phase
(osteoclasts remove micro-damaged bone) before *formation* — so during a rapid
build, bone can transiently weaken before it strengthens, creating a window for
stress reaction → stress fracture. This remodelling depends on adequate energy,
sex hormones (oestrogen, testosterone), calcium, and vitamin D. When **energy
availability is low**, the endocrine milieu shifts to energy conservation
(suppressed oestrogen/testosterone, altered IGF-1, leptin, thyroid), bone formation
falls, resorption persists, and bone-stress-injury risk rises sharply — the core of
**RED-S** and the female athlete triad [Mountjoy 2023].

**Why cadence matters biomechanically.** At a fixed pace, taking *more, shorter*
steps reduces stride length, moves footstrike closer to the body's centre of mass,
lowers vertical oscillation, and reduces the peak forces and loading rates at the
knee and hip per step. Increasing step rate ~5–10% measurably lowers
**patellofemoral joint force** and energy absorbed at the knee — directly relevant
to runner's knee and tibial stress [Lenhart 2014].

**Why strength helps.** Stronger muscle-tendon units have greater load-bearing
reserve, distribute force better, fatigue later (fatigue degrades running
mechanics), and stiffen tendons adaptively. Heavy, slow resistance training in
particular raises the capacity ceiling against which running load is compared
[Lauersen 2018].

## The evidence

**Epidemiology — how common, and where**
- **[Established]** Recreational runners sustain ~**7.7 RRIs per 1000 h** (95% CI
  6.9–8.7); **novices** ~**17.8/1000 h** (16.7–19.1) — novices are at markedly
  higher risk. *Systematic review + meta-analysis of 13 studies* [Videbæk 2015,
  *Sports Medicine*]. Consistent across cohorts; the headline numbers are robust,
  though absolute rates vary with how "injury" is defined.
- **[Established/Probable]** The knee is the most commonly injured region
  (~25–30%); patellofemoral pain, medial tibial stress syndrome, Achilles
  tendinopathy, plantar fasciitis and ITB syndrome dominate. *Systematic reviews of
  prospective cohorts* [Lopes 2012; Videbæk 2015]. Consistent.
- **[Established]** **Previous injury is the strongest single predictor of future
  RRI.** Multiple prospective cohorts converge: prior-year injury roughly doubles to
  triples risk (e.g. HR ~2.7; ORs ~1.9–2.5) [Saragiotto 2014 review; cohort data].
  Consistent and large — this is the most reliable individual risk factor known.

**Training load — the primary lever**
- **[Probable]** Large, abrupt increases in weekly running distance are associated
  with higher RRI risk. *Prospective cohort of 874 novices*: progressing weekly
  distance by **>30%** over 2 weeks was associated with more "distance-related"
  injuries (patellofemoral pain, ITBS, MTSS, etc.) than progressing <10%; the
  association was *injury-type specific*, not universal [Nielsen 2014, *JOSPT*].
  Observational, so confounded by who chooses to ramp — but biologically coherent
  and broadly replicated. This is the empirical core of "avoid load spikes."
- **[Contested]** **The "10% per week" rule.** The *only RCT to test it* — a graded
  13-week program built on the 10% rule vs a standard 8-week program in 532 novices
  — found **no difference in injury incidence** [Buist 2008, *AJSM*]. So 10% is a
  *reasonable conservative heuristic but is not a validated threshold*; it lacks
  RCT support and the exact percentage is arbitrary.
- **[Contested]** **Acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR).** Across sports a "sweet
  spot" of roughly **0.8–1.3** (and elevated risk above ~1.5) is frequently
  reported, and a recent meta-analysis supports an association with injury risk
  [Gabbett 2016; ACWR meta-analysis 2025]. **But** systematic reviews flag high
  heterogeneity in how ACWR is calculated and major methodological/statistical
  criticism (e.g. spurious correlation, arbitrary binning, the ratio being only
  weakly predictive once chronic load is accounted for) [Maupin 2020; Impellizzeri
  critique]. **Validated chiefly in team sports, not running specifically.** Treat
  ACWR as a *monitoring flag*, not a precise predictive instrument. See
  `training-load-acwr`.

**Modifiers — strength and cadence**
- **[Established]** **Strength training reduces injury risk.** *Meta-analysis of 25
  RCTs* across sports: strength training reduced overuse injuries to **<1/2** (and
  acute injuries substantially), outperforming stretching and proprioception
  [Lauersen 2014, *BJSM*]. A 2018 update found a **dose-response**: more strength
  training volume → fewer injuries, RR ≈ 0.34 [Lauersen 2018, *BJSM*]. Strong and
  consistent, though most trials are mixed-sport — running-specific RCTs are fewer.
- **[Probable]** In endurance runners specifically, exercise-based prevention
  programs showed **no overall effect**, but a pre-specified subgroup of
  ***supervised*** interventions *did* significantly reduce injury risk — supervision
  (and the adherence it buys) appears to matter. *Systematic review + meta-analysis,
  9 trials, 1904 runners* [Wu 2024, *Sports Medicine*]. Encouraging but heterogeneous
  and smaller than the cross-sport base.
- **[Probable]** **Increasing cadence ~5–10% reduces joint loading.** Controlled
  biomechanics: +10% step rate cut **peak patellofemoral force by ~14%** and reduced
  energy absorbed at the knee [Lenhart 2014, *MSSE*]. A *systematic review + meta-
  analysis* confirms **strong evidence for reduced peak force** with higher step
  rate, and *limited* clinical evidence that step-rate retraining improves pain and
  function in runners with patellofemoral pain [Anderson 2022, *Sports Med Open*].
  Mechanism solid; injury-*prevention* (vs treatment) evidence is thinner, and the
  effect is modest and pace-dependent. **Not** a universal "180 spm" target — see
  `cadence`.
- **[Myth]** **"Every runner should run at 180 spm."** The 180 figure came from
  Daniels' informal observation of elite distance runners *at race pace* and was
  never established as a universal optimum. Cadence scales with speed, height, and
  limb length; optimal step rate is **individual**, and the evidence supports a
  *relative* ~5–10% increase from a runner's own baseline where indicated — **not** a
  fixed 180 target for everyone [Anderson 2022; see `cadence`].

**Bone-stress injury & RED-S**
- **[Established]** **Low energy availability (LEA) impairs bone health and raises
  bone-stress-injury (BSI) risk.** *IOC consensus statement* synthesising the
  literature: chronic LEA disrupts the endocrine support for bone and is a primary
  driver of BSI and stress fractures [Mountjoy 2023, *BJSM*]. Between one-third and
  two-thirds of competitive endurance runners report a BSI history.
- **[Established/Probable]** **Menstrual dysfunction / low testosterone marks
  elevated bone risk.** In elite distance runners, amenorrhoea (≈37% of women) and
  low testosterone (≈40% of men) were associated with **~4.5× higher bone-injury
  rates** vs eumenorrhoeic/normal-testosterone peers [Mountjoy 2023 and cited
  cohorts]. Consistent; a powerful clinical flag.
- **[Probable]** Risk stratification matters: **high-risk BSI sites** (femoral neck,
  anterior tibial cortex, navicular, sacrum, base of 2nd metatarsal) carry worse
  prognosis and need imaging-confirmed healing; trabecular/low-risk sites
  (posteromedial tibia, fibula, metatarsal shafts) heal faster. Female runners and
  those with multiple RED-S risk factors cluster in the high-risk group [Tenforde
  2024, *OJSM*].

**Return to running**
- **[Probable / practitioner consensus]** After a tibial BSI, return-to-run should
  be **graduated and individualised**: confirm resolution of bony tenderness,
  pain-free walking, (for high-risk sites) radiological healing, and adequate
  strength/loading tolerance *before* running; then progress with **walk-run
  intervals, distance before speed/intensity, repeating each level**, governed by
  symptom provocation. The **10% rule is explicitly *not* generalisable** here, and
  **females may need a slower initial walk→run progression** (greater tibial-strain
  increase on that transition). *Scoping review* [George 2024, *Sports Medicine*].
  Evidence is low-level (consensus/protocols, not RCTs) but coherent and clinically
  standard.

## How we compute it
Daud does not measure tissue capacity, bone density, or energy availability
directly. The coach reasons from training-derived proxies and runner self-report:

- **Load progression (%)** — week-over-week change in volume (or load). `@daud/core`
  derives this from logged sessions. Used as the primary spike detector; ~>30%
  jumps flagged [Nielsen 2014].
- **ACWR** — acute (7-day) ÷ chronic (28-day, EWMA preferred) load. Owned by the
  load module (`training-load-acwr`). Treated as a *monitoring band* (~0.8–1.3
  "normal", >1.5 elevated), **not** a hard predictor — see the contested grade.
- **Cadence (spm)** — measured per session; deviation from the runner's own baseline
  matters more than any absolute target. See `cadence`.
- **Energy-availability / RED-S risk** — *not computed*; the coach flags from
  self-report (rapid weight loss, restrictive eating, menstrual changes, repeated
  bone injuries, persistent fatigue/illness). This is a screening flag, never a
  diagnosis.
- **Injury history** — logged prior injuries; the strongest individual risk weight
  the coach holds [Saragiotto 2014].

Flag: every numeric threshold here is a population starting point. Tissue capacity
and adaptation rate vary several-fold; the runner's own injury history and response
to load override the defaults.

## How the coach uses it
Decision logic, by **stage**:

**Load management (all stages)**
- Set each week's ceiling from *recent* training, not goal ambition. Prefer steady
  progression with periodic **down/recovery weeks** over monotonic ramps.
- Flag and discourage large spikes — a useful soft rule is **avoid >~10% sustained
  weekly increase and especially avoid >30% jumps** [Nielsen 2014; Buist 2008]; but
  present 10% as a *guideline*, not a guarantee, and individualise to history.
- Don't increase volume and intensity simultaneously; add one stressor at a time.
- After any break (illness, travel, layoff), **re-enter below** the pre-break load,
  not at it — chronic load has decayed.

**Strength & cadence (Stage 2–3, introduced gently in Stage 1)**
- Recommend **2× / week resistance training** (heavy, lower-limb-focused) as a
  first-line prevention tool — best-evidenced active intervention [Lauersen 2018].
- For runners with **patellofemoral or shin** problems, or who run at notably low
  cadence, suggest a **~5–10% cadence increase** with auditory cueing, retrained
  gradually — framed as load reduction, not a fixed 180 spm rule [Lenhart 2014;
  Anderson 2022; see `cadence`].

**Bone-stress / RED-S screening (all stages, safety-weighted)**
- Treat **repeated bone-stress injuries, restrictive eating, rapid weight loss,
  menstrual dysfunction (women), or low-libido/fatigue cluster (men)** as RED-S red
  flags → reduce load expectations and **refer to a sports physician / dietitian**.
  Do not "coach through" suspected under-fuelling [Mountjoy 2023].
- Pair any high-volume build with adequate fuelling (see `fueling-and-hydration`),
  sleep (see `sleep-and-recovery`), calcium/vitamin D sufficiency.

**Return-to-run (post-injury, safety-weighted)**
- After a bone-stress injury: **do not resume running** until bony tenderness has
  resolved, walking is pain-free, and (high-risk sites) healing is confirmed.
- Then use a **walk-run progression, distance before speed, repeating each level,
  symptom-guided**; back off on symptom provocation. Slower initial progression for
  females and high-risk sites [George 2024].

**Stage differences**
- **Stage 1 (beginner):** highest injury risk — bias hard toward conservative
  progression, run-walk starts, abundant easy running, and a low ceiling on weekly
  jumps; introduce light strength habits.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** systematise strength 2×/week, monitor load
  progression/ACWR as flags, address cadence/biomechanics if symptomatic.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** highest volumes/intensities → tightest bone-stress and
  RED-S vigilance, periodised loading with deliberate recovery weeks, individualised
  load ceilings from the runner's own injury-free history.

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **"Injury" is defined inconsistently.** Incidence numbers (7.7/1000 h etc.) shift
  with the definition used (any pain vs time-loss vs medical-attention). The
  *relative* ordering (novice ≫ recreational) is solid; treat absolute rates as
  ballpark [Videbæk 2015].
- **Most load evidence is observational.** The strongest practical lever — avoiding
  load spikes — rests largely on prospective cohorts [Nielsen 2014], not RCTs, and
  is confounded (runners who ramp may differ systematically). The one RCT of a
  load-progression rule (10%) was **null** [Buist 2008]. So we are confident in the
  *principle* (don't spike load) but genuinely uncertain about *precise numbers*.
- **The 10% rule and ACWR are over-sold.** Both are heuristics with weak or
  contested validation in running specifically. ACWR has serious statistical
  criticism and was developed in team sports; the coach must not present either as a
  precise injury predictor [Maupin 2020; Impellizzeri critique].
- **Cadence is a modifier, not a cure, and not "180 for everyone."** The
  biomechanical force reduction is real but modest and pace-dependent; *injury-
  prevention* (vs symptom-treatment) evidence is limited to a couple of studies
  [Anderson 2022]. Optimal cadence is individual.
- **Strength training evidence is strong but mostly cross-sport.** The headline
  "halves overuse injury" comes from mixed-sport RCTs; running-specific trials are
  fewer, so effect size in runners specifically is less precisely known [Lauersen
  2014/2018].
- **RED-S is real but hard to measure in-app.** Daud cannot measure energy
  availability; it can only screen from self-report and injury patterns, which miss
  cases and risk false alarms. The menstrual/testosterone–bone link is robust, but
  causation in any individual is multifactorial.
- **Individual variation dominates.** Anatomy, history, biomechanics, surface,
  footwear, sleep, stress, and genetics all modulate risk. Population thresholds are
  starting points to be refined from the runner's own response — never gospel.
- **What's still unknown:** the optimal load-progression rate (if a single number
  even exists), whether ACWR adds anything beyond simply "don't spike load,"
  the causal injury-prevention effect of cadence retraining, and how to individualise
  energy-availability thresholds.

## Safety bounds
Hard limits the coach must respect; safety-critical ones are mirrored as guardrails
in `@daud/core` and may not be overridden by the AI:

- **Suspected bone-stress injury / stress fracture** (localised bony pain,
  point tenderness, pain on hopping, pain that worsens through a run or persists at
  rest) → **stop running and refer to a medical professional.** Do not prescribe
  "run through it." (SAFETY-CRITICAL.)
- **Suspected RED-S / low energy availability** (restrictive eating, rapid weight
  loss, menstrual dysfunction, repeated bone-stress injuries, the low-energy
  symptom cluster) → **do not increase training; flag and refer** to a sports
  physician/dietitian. Never advise further energy restriction or weight loss for a
  performance goal in this context. (SAFETY-CRITICAL; cross-linked to RED-S handling
  in `fueling-and-hydration`.)
- **Post-bone-stress-injury return-to-run** must be gated on resolved bony
  tenderness and pain-free walking (and confirmed healing for high-risk sites)
  before *any* running, then graduated and symptom-guided. Do not apply a generic
  10%/week ramp to BSI rehab. (SAFETY-CRITICAL.)
- **Sharp, worsening, or one-sided pain that alters gait** → stop the session;
  pain that persists >48 h or recurs across sessions warrants assessment.
- **Never present the 10% rule or an ACWR band as a guarantee of safety** — they are
  guidelines, and a runner can be injured within them.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Running injury is common and overwhelmingly **overuse-driven**; novices and
  previously-injured runners are the highest-risk groups [Videbæk 2015; Saragiotto
  2014]. Injury history is the strongest individual predictor.
- **Avoiding large, abrupt load increases is the primary lever** — progress
  gradually, add one stressor at a time, use recovery weeks, re-enter low after
  breaks [Nielsen 2014].
- **Strength training (≈2×/week, lower-limb) meaningfully reduces overuse-injury
  risk** with a dose-response — the best-evidenced active prevention [Lauersen
  2014/2018].
- **Low energy availability / RED-S drives bone-stress injury**; menstrual
  dysfunction or low testosterone marks ~4.5× higher bone-injury risk — screen and
  refer, never coach through it [Mountjoy 2023].
- **Post-bone-stress-injury return must be graduated, walk-run, symptom-guided** —
  not rushed and not on a generic 10% ramp [George 2024].

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- The **exact** safe rate of load progression. "10%/week" is a sensible heuristic but
  failed its one RCT and has no validated precise threshold [Buist 2008].
- **ACWR** as a predictive instrument: associated with risk in team sports, but
  methodologically contested and not validated in running — use as a flag, not a law
  [Maupin 2020].
- **Cadence retraining for injury *prevention*** (vs treating existing knee/shin
  pain): mechanistically sound, modest, individual, thin prevention evidence — and
  definitely not a universal 180 spm rule [Anderson 2022].

## Coach Directives
- **D1:** Treat every runner as overuse-injury-prone; weight **prior injury history**
  heavily and bias new/returning runners toward conservative progression. —
  confidence: Established
- **D2:** Set each week's load ceiling from *recent* training; **avoid sustained
  weekly increases beyond ~10%, and especially avoid >30% jumps** in volume —
  presented as a guideline, not a guarantee. — confidence: Probable
- **D3:** Do not increase volume and intensity in the same step; add one stressor at
  a time, and schedule periodic recovery/down weeks. — confidence: Probable
- **D4:** After a layoff (illness/travel/break), **re-enter below** pre-break load —
  chronic load has decayed. — confidence: Probable
- **D5:** Recommend **lower-limb strength training ~2×/week** as first-line
  prevention; more is generally better (dose-response). — confidence: Established
- **D6:** For runners with patellofemoral/shin pain or notably low cadence, suggest a
  **gradual ~5–10% cadence increase** (auditory cueing), framed as joint-load
  reduction — **not** a fixed 180 spm target. — confidence: Probable
- **D7:** Use ACWR and 10%/week only as **monitoring flags**, never as precise
  predictors or safety guarantees; cross-reference `training-load-acwr`. —
  confidence: Contested
- **D8:** Screen for **RED-S/low energy availability** (restrictive eating, rapid
  weight loss, menstrual dysfunction, repeated bone-stress injuries, low-energy
  cluster); on any flag, **do not increase load and refer** to a sports
  physician/dietitian — never advise further restriction. — confidence: Established
  (SAFETY-CRITICAL, mirrored as a `@daud/core` guardrail)
- **D9:** On suspected **bone-stress injury** (localised bony tenderness, pain on
  hopping, pain worsening through/after a run or at rest), **stop running and refer**;
  do not advise running through it. — confidence: Established (SAFETY-CRITICAL)
- **D10:** Gate **return-to-run after a bone-stress injury** on resolved bony
  tenderness and pain-free walking (plus confirmed healing for high-risk sites), then
  use a **walk-run, distance-before-speed, repeat-each-level, symptom-guided**
  progression — slower for females and high-risk sites; do not apply a generic
  10%/week ramp. — confidence: Probable (SAFETY-CRITICAL)
- **D11:** Pair any high-volume build with adequate **fuelling, sleep, and
  calcium/vitamin D**; cross-link `fueling-and-hydration` and `sleep-and-recovery`. —
  confidence: Probable

## Healthee implementation & honesty policy
- **No `derived_daily` field of its own (`applies_to_metrics: []`).** Daud does not measure
  tissue capacity, bone density, or energy availability. The body's `@daud/core` quantities
  (`acwr` / `weeklyDistance` / `weeklyLoad` / `sessionLoad` / `cadence`, and load-progression
  %) are training-derived proxies and self-report screens, not Healthee `derived_daily`
  fields created by this note; ACWR itself is owned by `training_load_acwr`.
- **Cross-link (kept separate, complementary):** the load-progression / ACWR framing lives in
  the reconciled `training_load_acwr`; this note is the *injury* application (bone-stress,
  RED-S, strength/cadence). Cite both, don't duplicate.
- **Best-evidenced levers + safety-critical guardrails to mirror in code (not
  LLM-overridable):**
  - **Suspected bone-stress injury** (localised bony tenderness, pain on hopping, pain
    worsening through/after a run or at rest) → **stop running and refer** (D9,
    SAFETY-CRITICAL); **return-to-run after a BSI** is gated on resolved tenderness +
    pain-free walking (+ confirmed healing for high-risk sites), then walk-run,
    distance-before-speed, symptom-guided, slower for females/high-risk sites — **not** a
    generic 10%/week ramp (D10, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
  - **Screen for RED-S / low energy availability** (restrictive eating, rapid weight loss,
    menstrual dysfunction, repeated bone-stress injuries) → **do not increase load; flag and
    refer**; never advise further restriction (D8, SAFETY-CRITICAL; cross-link
    `fueling_and_hydration`, `menstrual_cycle_and_training`).
  - First-line active prevention is **lower-limb strength ~2×/week** (halves overuse risk,
    dose-dependent) and a **~5–10% cadence increase** for knee/shin problems — framed as load
    reduction, **not** a fixed 180 spm rule.
- **Honesty rules (carry into UI + LLM):**
  - Be confident in the **principle** (don't spike load; strength prevents injury; LEA drives
    bone-stress injury) but honest that **precise numbers are weak** — the **10% rule failed
    its one RCT** and **ACWR is contested and team-sport-derived** (monitoring flags, never
    guarantees of safety) (D2, D7).
  - Every threshold is a **population starting point**; **prior injury is the strongest
    individual predictor** — weight the runner's own history over any population number.
