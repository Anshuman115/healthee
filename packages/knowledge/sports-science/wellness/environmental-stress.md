# Heat & Altitude

## Summary
Heat and altitude are the two environmental stressors that most distort a runner's
pace, heart rate, and physiology — and both can be turned from a handicap into an
adaptation. **Heat acclimatization is one of the best-evidenced interventions in
endurance sport:** ~10–14 days of heat exposure expands plasma volume, lowers
heart rate (~11 bpm) and core temperature (~0.3 °C), raises sweat rate, and
improves performance in the heat by ~15–23% [Périard 2015; Tyler 2016
meta-analysis]. In the heat itself, the coach must read **effort and pace
alongside heart rate**, because cardiovascular drift inflates HR at any given
pace. **Altitude via "live high, train low" (LHTL)** can raise red-cell mass and
sea-level performance by ~1–4% in responders, but the effect is smaller, more
variable, dose-dependent (~2000–2500 m, ≥3–4 weeks, ≥22 h/day), and genuinely
contested once placebo-controlled — far less settled than heat. Both stressors
carry real safety tails: exertional heat illness and acute altitude illness.

## What it is
Two distinct environmental loads the coach must reason about:

- **Heat stress** — the combined thermal load of air temperature, humidity, solar
  radiation, and wind that impairs the body's ability to dissipate the heat it
  produces while running. Captured crudely by air temperature, better by **dew
  point** (a direct humidity proxy) or **WBGT** (wet-bulb globe temperature, the
  standard composite index). Humidity matters enormously: at high humidity, sweat
  cannot evaporate, so the body's main cooling route fails even at moderate
  temperatures.
- **Heat acclimatization vs acclimation** — *acclimatization* = adaptation from
  natural environmental heat exposure; *acclimation* = adaptation induced in a
  controlled setting (heat chamber, hot room, sauna, overdressing). Physiologically
  similar; this doc uses "heat acclimation/HA" for both.
- **Altitude / hypoxia** — reduced barometric pressure at elevation lowers the
  partial pressure of oxygen, reducing arterial oxygen saturation and aerobic
  capacity. Relevant bands: **low** (<1200 m, negligible), **moderate**
  (~1500–2500 m, the LHTL "live" zone), **high** (2500–3500 m), **very high**
  (>3500 m, illness risk rises steeply).
- **Live High, Train Low (LHTL)** — live/sleep at moderate altitude to drive
  erythropoietic adaptation, but do hard training near sea level so workout quality
  and oxygen flux are preserved. The intended best-of-both protocol.

## Physiology / mechanism

**Heat — the problem.** Running converts only ~20–25% of metabolic energy into
movement; the rest is heat. To stay near 37 °C core, the body shunts blood to the
skin and sweats. In a hot/humid environment two things compete for a finite
cardiac output: working muscle (needs oxygen) and skin (needs heat dumped). Skin
blood flow rises, central blood volume and stroke volume fall, and heart rate
climbs to compensate — **cardiovascular drift**. When evaporation is blocked by
high humidity, core temperature rises, and at a critical core temperature
(~~39.5–40 °C) the central nervous system reduces drive and pace collapses
("hyperthermia-induced central fatigue"). This is why heat hurts endurance far
more than it hurts a 400 m.

**Heat — the adaptation.** Repeated heat exposure triggers a coordinated set of
changes [Périard 2015]:
- **Plasma volume expansion** (the fastest and arguably most important): blood
  volume rises ~3–15% within the first ~3–6 days via fluid retention
  (aldosterone/sodium conservation, increased plasma protein). More plasma → better
  stroke volume → lower HR and more cardiovascular reserve.
- **Lower heart rate** at any given work rate (less cardiovascular strain).
- **Lower resting and exercising core temperature**, and a higher core-temp
  threshold for the same strain.
- **Earlier onset and higher rate of sweating**, with **more dilute sweat** (sodium
  conserved via aldosterone-driven reabsorption) — better evaporative cooling, less
  electrolyte loss.
- **Improved skin blood flow** and cellular heat-shock protein protection.

**Altitude — the problem and the adaptation.** Lower inspired oxygen reduces
arterial saturation and thus VO₂max (~roughly 1–2% per 100 m above ~1500 m). The
body senses hypoxia and the kidneys release **erythropoietin (EPO)** within hours
(EPO can roughly double ~20 h after ascent), stimulating red-cell production over
weeks. The result, if sustained, is increased **red-cell / hemoglobin mass**,
raising oxygen-carrying capacity and — back at sea level — VO₂max and endurance.
There are also debated *non-hematological* adaptations (muscle buffering, economy,
mitochondrial efficiency). The catch: **training hard at altitude is compromised**
because hypoxia forces slower paces and lower power, so absolute training stimulus
drops — the rationale for living high but training low.

## The evidence

Heat acclimation is among the most conclusively established interventions in the
environmental-physiology literature; altitude/LHTL is real but markedly weaker and
contested. Graded accordingly.

### Heat

- **[Established]** Heat acclimation reliably improves thermoregulation and
  performance in the heat — *meta-analysis* [Tyler 2016, *Sports Medicine*, 96
  studies]: following HA, exercising core temperature fell ~0.31 °C, heart rate
  fell ~11 bpm, sweat rate rose, and **exercise performance/capacity in the heat
  improved by ~15–23%** on average. Consistent across studies; effect sizes
  moderate-to-large. This is the anchor finding.
- **[Established]** Plasma volume expands early and substantially with heat
  exposure — *narrative review of decades of controlled work* [Périard 2015,
  *Scand J Med Sci Sports*]: PV rises within the first few days, underpinning the
  early HR reduction; most thermoregulatory adaptations are largely complete by
  ~7–14 days of daily exposure.
- **[Established]** Acclimatization is the single most effective countermeasure for
  performing and staying safe in the heat — *expert consensus statement* [Racinais
  2015, *Br J Sports Med*]: recommends repeated exercise-heat exposures over ~1–2
  weeks before hot competition; prolonged (not short) events are where heat most
  impairs performance.
- **[Established]** Heat degrades endurance performance in a dose-dependent way with
  environmental load — *large observational dataset* [Ely 2007, *Med Sci Sports
  Exerc*, 7 marathons, decades of finish times]: progressive slowing of marathon
  times as WBGT rises from ~5 to 25 °C; slower runners are penalized more in
  relative terms. Optimal endurance temperature is cool (~10–12 °C for elites).
- **[Probable]** Short-term HA (≤7 days) captures most of the cardiovascular
  benefit (plasma volume, HR), while a fuller thermoregulatory adaptation (sweat,
  core-temp defense) continues to ~10–14 days — *consistent across controlled
  studies* [Périard 2015]. Hence the "10–14 day" rule of thumb, with meaningful
  gains already by day 5–7.
- **[Contested]** Whether heat acclimation improves performance in *cool*
  conditions (a "poor man's altitude camp" via plasma-volume-driven VO₂max gains):
  the influential *single study* [Lorenzo 2010, *J Appl Physiol*, n=12 cyclists]
  found ~5% higher VO₂max and ~6% better time-trial in the cool after 10 days HA —
  but a *direct attempted replication* [Karlsen 2015, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*]
  found **no** VO₂max or cool-weather performance benefit in trained cyclists.
  Findings genuinely conflict; treat cross-over-to-cool benefit as unproven.
- **[Probable]** Heat adaptations **decay** without continued exposure — *consistent
  but less precisely quantified*: plasma-volume and HR benefits fade over ~1–4
  weeks once heat exposure stops (rough practitioner rule: ~1 day of decay per ~2
  days of acclimation; faster for sweat adaptations). Periodic "top-up" exposures
  preserve them [Périard 2015].

### Altitude

- **[Established]** Moderate hypoxia rapidly raises EPO and, sustained for weeks,
  increases red-cell/hemoglobin mass — *seminal controlled study* [Levine &
  Stray-Gundersen 1997, *J Appl Physiol*]: this is the physiological basis of
  altitude training and is not in dispute.
- **[Probable]** "Live high, train low" improves sea-level endurance performance in
  elite/sub-elite runners — *controlled trial* [Stray-Gundersen 2001, *J Appl
  Physiol*, 14 men + 8 women elite runners]: 27 days living at 2500 m / training at
  1250 m improved sea-level 3000 m time by ~1.1%, alongside red-cell-mass and
  VO₂max gains. *Meta-analysis* [Bonetti & Hopkins 2009, *Sports Medicine*]: natural
  LHTL produced ~4% mean performance gain in sub-elite and elite, with wide spread
  (~0.3–7.7%). Real but modest, with large between-athlete variability.
- **[Probable]** There is an altitude **dose** for the hematological response —
  *controlled dose-finding study* [Chapman 2014, *J Appl Physiol*, 48 collegiate
  runners across four living altitudes 1780–2800 m]: consistent erythropoietic
  acclimatization centred on living at **~2000–2500 m** for **≥3–4 weeks** at **≥22
  h/day**; too low gives no stimulus, too high impairs sleep, training and recovery.
  Even at an "optimal" altitude, EPO and red-cell-mass responses vary substantially
  between individuals — **responders and non-responders are real**.
- **[Contested]** Whether LHTL's benefit is truly hematological — or substantially
  placebo/training-camp effect — once properly blinded: *double-blind,
  placebo-controlled study* [Siebenmann 2012, *J Appl Physiol*, normobaric hypoxia,
  16 h/day at 3000 m for 4 weeks] found **no** increase in hemoglobin mass and
  **no** detectable performance benefit versus a sham-air control. Normobaric
  ("simulated") altitude at lower daily hours is weaker than natural terrestrial
  altitude, and blinding strips out expectation effects — so the magnitude, and
  sometimes the existence, of the LHTL benefit is legitimately debated. Natural
  LHTL at sufficient dose has the strongest support; simulated/low-dose protocols
  much weaker.
- **[Emerging/Contested]** "Live high, train high" and "live low, train high"
  (intermittent hypoxic exposure/training) are weaker still — intermittent exposure
  shows little benefit in elites; LHTL remains the best-supported model [Bonetti &
  Hopkins 2009].
- **[Myth]** "Altitude training reliably makes everyone faster." Refuted as stated —
  the mean effect is small (~1–4%), highly individual, dose-dependent, and shrinks
  or disappears under placebo control. It is a tool with responders, not a guarantee
  [Siebenmann 2012; Chapman 2014].

## How we compute it

Daud does not measure core temperature, plasma volume, or red-cell mass. The coach
works from environment inputs and the runner's response:

- **Heat load** is taken from weather data: air temperature (°C), relative humidity
  (%), and ideally **dew point** (the best single, widely available humidity/heat
  proxy) or WBGT. `@daud/core` `heatStressIndex()` (or equivalent) maps
  (tempC, humidity, [solar/wind]) → a heat-stress band the coach reasons with.
  Not yet a calibrated physiological model — it is an environmental classifier.
- **Heat pace/HR adjustment** is an estimate, not a measurement. As a starting
  rule, expect HR elevated ~10 bpm or more at a given pace in significant heat, and
  expect easy/threshold paces to slow. The engine should anchor easy and tempo runs
  to **effort/HR (run by feel), not the cool-weather pace target**, in heat. Slowdown
  magnitude is approximated from the WBGT/marathon literature (~roughly 0.3–0.4%
  per °C of marathon-paced slowing above the optimum [Ely 2007]) but must be refined
  per runner.
- **Acclimatization state** is tracked behaviorally: count of heat exposures in the
  last ~2 weeks and the trend in HR-at-pace / aerobic decoupling in the heat. A
  falling HR-at-pace across repeated hot sessions is the observable signature of
  adaptation; Daud infers it rather than measuring plasma volume.
- **Altitude** is taken from elevation (m). The coach flags acute-altitude
  performance/illness risk and, for athletes deliberately doing LHTL, reasons about
  dose (altitude, days, hours/day) against the literature — it does not predict an
  individual's red-cell-mass gain (too variable to compute).

Flag: every number here is a population starting point. The runner's own logged
HR-at-effort in heat, perceived exertion, and historical altitude response override
the defaults.

## How the coach uses it

**In the heat — the core rule: trust effort over pace, and read HR with care.**
- Coach easy and long runs by **perceived effort and heart rate**, and explicitly
  permit slower paces in heat/humidity. Do not flag a hot-day pace as "lost
  fitness." Cross-check: in heat, HR drifts up at a fixed pace (decoupling), so a
  high HR alone is not overtraining — weigh pace, effort, and conditions together.
- For quality sessions in heat, prefer **intensity by feel / HR or by adjusted
  pace**, not absolute cool-weather pace targets, to avoid overcooking the runner.
- Prefer cooler parts of the day, shade, and pre-/mid-run cooling for hard efforts.
- Reinforce hydration guidance from `fueling-and-hydration` (drink to thirst; sweat
  losses rise sharply in heat) — but never coach drinking *ahead* of thirst
  (hyponatremia risk).

**Deliberate heat acclimatization (when a hot race or summer block is coming).**
- Prescribe **repeated heat exposures over ~10–14 days** (daily or near-daily),
  ~60 min of exercise in the heat per session, beginning ~2 weeks before a hot
  target event [Périard 2015; Racinais 2015]. Expect noticeable benefit by day 5–7.
- Start conservatively (lower intensity/duration), progress as HR-at-effort drops.
- After the block, schedule periodic **top-up** heat exposures (e.g. every ~3–5
  days) to offset decay if the race is weeks away.
- Frame the gain honestly: large for performing *in the heat*; do **not** promise a
  cool-weather performance boost (that cross-over is contested).

**Altitude / LHTL (advanced, Stage 3, and only when feasible).**
- LHTL is an elite/committed-athlete tool, not a default. If pursued, target the
  evidence-based dose: **live ~2000–2500 m, train low, ≥3–4 weeks, ≥22 h/day**
  [Chapman 2014]. Lower altitude or fewer hours markedly weakens the stimulus.
- Set expectations explicitly: a realistic *mean* sea-level gain of ~1–4%, with a
  real chance of **no** response (responders vs non-responders) [Bonetti & Hopkins
  2009; Siebenmann 2012]. Ensure iron status is adequate first (red-cell production
  needs iron).
- On arrival at altitude (or for a runner who lives/visits high), expect impaired
  paces and higher HR; reduce intensity for the first several days and judge effort
  by feel, not sea-level pace.

**Stage differences**
- **Stage 1 (beginner):** keep it simple — in heat, slow down and run by feel,
  hydrate to thirst, avoid the hottest hours. No structured acclimatization or
  altitude protocols.
- **Stage 2 (developing):** introduce deliberate heat acclimatization before a hot
  goal race; track HR-at-effort to confirm adaptation.
- **Stage 3 (racing):** full ~10–14 day heat-acclimatization blocks with top-ups
  timed to the race; consider LHTL only with proper dose, iron screening, and
  calibrated (non-guaranteed) expectations.

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **Heat adaptation is robust; the cool-weather carry-over is not.** The headline HA
  performance/physiology benefits *in the heat* are meta-analysis-grade and settled
  [Tyler 2016]. The popular idea that HA also boosts cool-weather VO₂max/performance
  rests on one positive study [Lorenzo 2010] that a direct replication failed to
  confirm [Karlsen 2015] — the coach must not sell it as established.
- **Altitude is the genuinely contested topic here.** The physiology (EPO → red-cell
  mass) is real, but the *performance* payoff is small, highly individual, and
  shrinks or vanishes under double-blind placebo control [Siebenmann 2012]. Natural
  terrestrial LHTL at full dose has the best evidence; simulated/normobaric and
  low-dose protocols are weak. Expectation/placebo and training-camp effects are
  large confounders.
- **Huge individual variation.** Sweat rate, sweat sodium, plasma-volume
  responsiveness, and especially the erythropoietic response to altitude vary
  several-fold. Population means are starting estimates, not personal predictions —
  some athletes are clear altitude non-responders.
- **HR misleads in heat.** Cardiovascular drift inflates HR at a given pace, so HR
  zones drift in heat; a "too-high" HR in heat is often environmental, not fitness
  loss or overreaching. This is exactly why effort/pace must be weighted alongside
  HR (see `aerobic-decoupling`, `heart-rate-zones`).
- **Decay is real but imprecisely quantified.** Heat adaptations fade over ~1–4
  weeks without exposure; the exact decay curve is individual, so top-up cadence is
  an estimate to refine.
- **Heat and altitude interact and stack other stressors.** Both raise resting HR,
  perturb sleep (altitude especially), and add physiological load on top of training
  — the coach should down-weight training stress when either is present.
- **What's unknown:** the precise per-runner heat-decay curve, who will respond to
  altitude (no reliable pre-test), the true size of any non-hematological altitude
  benefit, and whether HA's cool-weather carry-over exists at all.

## Safety bounds
These are hard limits the coach must respect; safety-critical ones are mirrored as
guardrails in `@daud/core` and may not be overridden by the AI:

- **Exertional heat illness is a medical emergency.** Red-flag symptoms during/after
  hot exercise — confusion, disorientation, irritability, stumbling, collapse,
  nausea/vomiting, hot/dry *or* heavily sweating skin with altered behavior — point
  to possible exertional heat stroke. The coach must direct the runner to **stop,
  cool aggressively, and seek urgent medical care**, never give a pacing tip
  [Racinais 2015]. Heat stroke is life-threatening and survival depends on rapid
  cooling.
- **In dangerous heat, modify or cancel.** In extreme WBGT/heat-and-humidity, the
  coach must scale back intensity/duration, move the session cooler, or recommend
  postponement — especially for unacclimatized runners, who are at far higher risk.
- **Never coach drinking ahead of thirst, even in heat.** Over-drinking causes
  exercise-associated hyponatremia, which can be fatal (see `fueling-and-hydration`).
  Heat raises fluid needs but thirst remains the governing signal.
- **Altitude illness is a medical issue.** Symptoms of acute mountain sickness
  (worsening headache, nausea, dizziness, poor sleep) and especially the
  emergencies HAPE/HACE (severe breathlessness at rest, cough, confusion, ataxia)
  require **descent and medical care**, not training. Risk rises with altitude
  (notably >2500–3000 m) and rate of ascent; the coach must not push training into
  altitude-illness symptoms [Chapman 2014; consensus practice].
- **Screen iron before recommending altitude training.** Erythropoiesis requires
  adequate iron; iron-deficient athletes may not respond and can be harmed by the
  added demand.

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Heat acclimatization works and is well established — ~10–14 days of repeated heat
  exposure expands plasma volume, lowers HR (~11 bpm) and core temperature
  (~0.3 °C), raises/dilutes sweating, and improves performance *in the heat* by
  ~15–23% [Tyler 2016 meta-analysis; Périard 2015].
- Heat degrades endurance performance in proportion to environmental load, so in
  heat the coach should coach by **effort and HR, expect slower paces, and not read
  a hot-day slowdown as lost fitness** [Ely 2007; Racinais 2015].
- For a hot goal race, a ~10–14 day acclimatization block (≈60 min/day in heat,
  starting ~2 weeks out) with periodic top-ups is the highest-value intervention
  [Racinais 2015].
- Exertional heat illness and altitude illness are medical emergencies that override
  any training objective. (Safety-critical.)

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- That heat acclimation also improves **cool-weather** performance — one positive
  study, one failed replication; do not promise it [Lorenzo 2010 vs Karlsen 2015].
- That altitude/LHTL will make a given runner faster — physiology is real, but the
  mean gain is small (~1–4%), highly individual, dose-dependent, and contested under
  placebo control; expect responders and non-responders [Bonetti & Hopkins 2009;
  Chapman 2014; Siebenmann 2012].
- The exact decay rate of heat adaptations and the optimal top-up cadence —
  individualize from the runner's HR-at-effort trend.

- **No `derived_daily` field of its own (`applies_to_metrics: []`).** Daud does not measure
  core temperature, plasma volume, or red-cell mass. The body's `@daud/core` quantities
  (`environmentTempC` / `relativeHumidity` / `dewPointC` / `wbgt` / `altitudeM` /

  state is tracked *behaviorally* (count of heat exposures in the last ~2 weeks + the trend in
  HR-at-pace / aerobic decoupling), never by measuring plasma volume.
- **Cross-link (kept separate, complementary):** heat *training* adaptation here vs sauna →
  **mortality / cardiovascular** benefit in `sauna_cv_benefits` — same exposure (heat),
  different endpoint (performance-in-heat vs long-term CV/health). Do not conflate the two
  evidence bases.

    collapse, disorientation, vomiting, altered behavior) during/after hot exercise, **stop
    training advice and direct to immediate cooling + urgent medical care** (D12,
    SAFETY-CRITICAL).
  - In **extreme heat/WBGT, scale back, move cooler, or postpone**, especially for
    unacclimatized runners (D11, SAFETY-CRITICAL); on **altitude-illness** symptoms
    (worsening headache, severe breathlessness, confusion, ataxia) advise **descent and
    medical care** (D13, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
  - **Never coach drinking ahead of thirst, even in heat** (hyponatremia risk) — defer to
    `fueling_and_hydration` (D14, SAFETY-CRITICAL).
- **Honesty rules (carry into UI + LLM):**
  - **Heat acclimatization in the heat is Established and stated plainly** (~10–14 days;
    ~11 bpm HR, ~0.3 °C core, ~15–23% performance); its **cool-weather carry-over is
    Contested** (one positive study, one failed replication) and **altitude/LHTL is the
    genuinely contested topic** (~1–4% mean, real responders/non-responders, shrinks under
    placebo control) — never promise either.
  - **HR misleads in heat** (cardiovascular drift inflates HR at a fixed pace) — weight
    effort/pace alongside HR; do not read a hot-day slowdown as lost fitness (see
    `aerobic-decoupling`). Every number here is a population starting point to refine from the
    runner's own HR-at-effort trend.
