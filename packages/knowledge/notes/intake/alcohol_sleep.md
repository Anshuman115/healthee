## Finding
Even moderate alcohol consumption (1–2 standard drinks) **before bed**
predictably alters sleep architecture and autonomic nervous system markers
overnight:
1. Increases slow-wave sleep in the **first half** of the night, then **fragments** the second half (more wake, less REM).
2. Acutely **reduces HRV** (RMSSD and HF power) for the night and into the next morning.
3. Elevates **resting heart rate** for several hours overnight.

Effect is dose-dependent and detectable down to ~0.5 g alcohol/kg body weight
(roughly 1 standard drink in a 70-kg adult).

## Effect size
- Reduction in REM in second half: ~10–25% vs alcohol-free night (varies by dose).
- Acute drop in nocturnal HRV (RMSSD): commonly 15–30% on drinking nights vs non-drinking nights (consumer-wearable replications: e.g., Pietilä J et al. 2018).
- RHR elevation: ~5–10 bpm above personal baseline overnight, persisting until late morning.

## Evidence strength
- Ebrahim IO, Shapiro CM, Williams AJ, Fenwick PB. **"Alcohol and sleep I: effects on normal sleep."** *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research* 2013;37(4):539–549. Systematic review across multiple controlled-dose studies.
- Pietilä J, Helander E, Korhonen I, et al. **"Acute effect of alcohol intake on cardiovascular autonomic regulation during the first hours of sleep in a large real-world sample of Finnish employees: observational study."** *JMIR Mental Health* 2018;5(1):e23. n ≈ 4,098, consumer-wearable HRV data.
- Park SY et al. **"The effects of alcohol on quality of sleep."** *Korean Journal of Family Medicine* 2015;36(6):294–299. Review with focus on architecture changes.

## Caveats
- Tolerance / individual variation is real; some people show smaller effects, but the *direction* of effect is consistent.
- Light drinkers experience a relatively larger HRV impact than heavy drinkers (paradoxical, but replicated).
- Time-of-consumption matters: alcohol >4 hours before bed has smaller second-half effects.

## Operational use
- A drop in nightly HRV the morning after an alcohol entry is **expected**, not a health-concerning anomaly.
- Surface this connection when the user has logged alcohol and we observe an HRV/RHR shift the next morning — citing this note as the basis.
- Do not extrapolate to long-term health claims from a single drinking event.
