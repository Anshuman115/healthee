## Finding
Nightly HRV (RMSSD) reflects cardiac **vagal (parasympathetic) tone**, and it is
modifiable on two timescales:

- **Chronic (weeks–months) — raises the personal baseline.** The largest
  controllable lever is **regular aerobic exercise / improved cardiorespiratory
  fitness**. **Slow paced breathing (resonance-frequency ~6 breaths/min) / HRV
  biofeedback** also raises resting HRV with practice, and acutely boosts HRV
  during the session. Adequate, regular **sleep** supports a higher baseline.
- **Acute (same night) — suppresses HRV.** **Evening alcohol** is the single most
  reproducible suppressor (dose-dependent). **Short or fragmented sleep**, a hard
  training session the day before, **late caffeine**, late heavy meals, and acute
  psychological stress all push the autonomic balance toward sympathetic
  dominance and lower that night's RMSSD.

So a single low night (like a drop below the personal baseline) is usually an
*acute* signal — look at the prior day's alcohol / sleep / training / stress —
while sustained low HRV is what the chronic levers move.

## Effect size
- **Aerobic exercise training (chronic):** meta-analysis of RCTs in healthy adults —
  **RMSSD SMD ≈ 0.84** and HF-power SMD ≈ 0.89 vs controls (a *large* increase in
  vagally-mediated HRV); SDNN SMD ≈ 0.58. Strongest evidence-based way to raise the
  baseline.
- **Slow breathing / HRV biofeedback (chronic):** **small-to-moderate** effect on
  resting HRV (pooled g ≈ 0.2–0.3); much larger *acute* RMSSD increase while
  breathing at resonance frequency (~4.5–7 breaths/min, individually ~6). Best
  thought of as a daily practice, not a one-off.
- **Evening alcohol (acute, dose-dependent):** during the first hours of sleep,
  RMSSD fell by **≈ 2 ms (low dose), ≈ 6 ms (moderate), ≈ 13 ms (high)**; the effect
  is **larger in younger adults** (≈ −11 ms for a high dose at age 30 vs −5 ms at 60),
  alongside elevated heart rate. A common cause of an isolated low-HRV night.
- **Short / disrupted sleep (acute→chronic):** meta-analysis — sleep deprivation
  **lowers RMSSD and raises LF/HF** (sympathetic predominance). Short habitual sleep
  is associated with chronically reduced parasympathetic tone.

## Evidence strength
- **Exercise:** *"Effects of Exercise Training on Heart Rate Variability in Healthy Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials."* 2024. PMID **39015867**. (RCT meta-analysis; RMSSD SMD 0.84.)
- **Slow breathing / biofeedback:** Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R. *"Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work?"* Frontiers in Psychology 2014;5:756 (mechanism, resonance frequency). Shaffer F, Meehan ZM. *"A Practical Guide to Resonance Frequency Assessment for HRV Biofeedback."* Frontiers in Neuroscience 2020;14:570400 (PMC7793964). Pooled chronic effect small–moderate.
- **Alcohol:** Pietilä J, et al. *"Acute Effect of Alcohol Intake on Cardiovascular Autonomic Regulation During the First Hours of Sleep…"* JMIR Mental Health 2018;5(1):e23 (PMC5878366; large real-world sample, dose–response). Spaak J, et al. *"Dose-related effects of red wine and alcohol on heart rate variability."* Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 2010.
- **Sleep:** *"Effects of sleep deprivation on heart rate variability: a systematic review and meta-analysis."* Frontiers in Neurology 2025 (PMC12394884).

## Caveats
- **n=1 and acute noise:** a single low-HRV night carries little signal on its own;
  judge against a **personal rolling baseline (e.g. 7- and 28-day)** and pair it with
  the prior day's behaviours. See [[hrv_recovery_marker]].
- **Absolute values aren't comparable between people**, and strap/wrist HRV trends are
  reliable while exact ms values are not. Compare like-for-like (during sleep).
- **Chronic vs acute must not be conflated:** exercise/breathing move the baseline over
  weeks; alcohol/sleep move a single night. Don't promise a one-night fix from a
  chronic intervention, or treat one bad night as a baseline change.
- **Overtraining caveat:** exercise *raises* HRV chronically, but an acute hard session
  can lower HRV the following night — that's expected recovery, not harm.
- Dose-response for breathing practice (minutes/day, weeks-to-effect) is only loosely
  established; "10–20 min/day of ~6 breaths-per-minute breathing" is a pragmatic, not
  precisely-calibrated, dose.

## Operational use
- When HRV drops **below the personal baseline**, surface the most likely *acute*
  cause from logged/derived data first — **evening alcohol, short or poor sleep, a hard
  workout the prior day, late caffeine** — rather than alarming on the number alone.
- To **raise the baseline**, the coach/recs/challenges should prioritise, in order of
  evidence: (1) **regular aerobic exercise** (zone-2 / MVPA volume); (2) **consistent,
  adequate sleep** (especially relevant for a habitual short sleeper — their largest
  lever); (3) a **daily slow-breathing practice** (~6 breaths/min, 10–20 min) as a
  low-cost add-on; (4) **reducing evening alcohol**.
- Frame breathing/biofeedback as **moderate-confidence** for chronic baseline change but
  reliable for acute, in-session vagal activation.
- Always cite this note when recommending an HRV-improvement action, and pair the
  suggestion with the user's own data (their baseline, their logged alcohol/sleep).
